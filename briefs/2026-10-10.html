The story

The Associated Press headlined Friday “Wall Street finishes its record-setting week with gains”: the S&P 500 rose 0.59% to 7,811.54, its first gain since its Oct 6 record. CNBC led with the Dow’s 400-point rise and a winning week. The day’s biggest single-stock story was telecom. CNBC tied a sharp selloff in T-Mobile, Verizon and AT&T to SpaceX’s deal, announced Thursday evening and pending FCC approval, to buy 800 MHz spectrum from Grain Management for its Starlink Mobile service. Separately, Bloomberg reported Friday that OpenAI expects at least $70 billion in annualized revenue by the end of 2026, after Thursday’s reporting on a lower figure. Reuters had oil settling slightly higher, with Gulf of Mexico producers shutting in about 63% of output as of Thursday ahead of Hurricane Isaias, and gains pared after President Trump pledged no attack on Iran before the Nov. 3 midterms.

Does this system’s own data confirm that framing? On stocks, yes, and broadly: the equal-weight S&P proxy (RSP, the average stock) rose 0.55%, about in line with SPY’s +0.60%, and the S&P 500 ended the week up 1.15%. On tech, only partly. The Nasdaq rose 0.64%, but the chip fund SMH fell 0.65%, its fifth straight session behind SPY, and it lost 4.32% on the week. No major outlet gave a reason for the chip lag that this system could find, so none is claimed. On rates, the 10-year rose 2bp to 5.24%. It first reached that level on Sep 28 and has been there on 9 of the 10 sessions since; before this run, it last got there only briefly, in June 2007 (Today in history, below). Oil: WTI rose 0.39% to $91.85, matching Reuters’ settle; the reasons are Reuters’, untested here.

The new piece is credit. This morning’s pull filled in Thursday’s high-yield spread (HY OAS, §5): it widened 6bp to 3.15%, after 3.09% the day before. That refutes CL-0036 (Claims, above) and gives a clear answer to what Thursday’s brief said would be the real test: on the selloff day, junk-bond spreads widened while investment-grade spreads held flat. A new claim, CL-0041, tests whether that widening holds. Friday’s own credit print arrives on a later run.

Friday’s one scheduled release missed: the University of Michigan’s preliminary October consumer sentiment index came in at 46.3 (Bloomberg; the median forecast in Bloomberg’s survey was 47.6), down from 48.1 in September. Year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 4.7% from 4.6%. Bloomberg and others called the current-conditions reading (44.7) the lowest on record; this system holds no sentiment history to check that (§4F), so it stays their framing.

Concept of the day · Unit 5 — The dollar cycle, segment 4

The dollar moves in long waves: read today's level against the whole cycle

The first three segments covered how a strong dollar tightens conditions, why carry pulls money toward higher U.S. rates, and how that tightening spreads abroad. Today's piece is about time scale. The dollar doesn't swing day to day the way stocks do; it moves in waves that last years. So two yardsticks answer two different questions. The 120-day z-score (+2.04 on Friday) says the dollar is strong compared with the past six months. The long record says where that sits in the cycle. Since 1971, DXY (the dollar against a basket of major currencies) has ranged from 71.33, its 2000s low, to 164.72, its 1980s high. Friday's 102.21 is the 72nd percentile of that whole history and the 81st of the last 30 years. By decade, the dollar was at or above Friday's level on 57.7% of days in the 1980s, but on only 1.6% of days in the 1990s and 0.6% in the 2010s. Its latest peak, 114.11 in September 2022, was well above today. Put together: strong by recent memory, inside the long range. That matters for clients with international funds, since a dollar this strong shrinks what foreign holdings are worth in dollars, and the long record says it has gone both higher and much lower.

Client translation: The dollar moves in long waves: it's strong right now for anyone who remembers the 2010s, but ordinary for anyone who remembers the '80s, and over years it can swing a long way in either direction.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
