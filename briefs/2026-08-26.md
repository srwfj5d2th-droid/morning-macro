## The story

This morning's headline is trade policy, not markets. Canada announced retaliatory tariffs of 15%, 25% and 50% on roughly $20 billion of U.S. goods — steel, dairy, appliances, farm equipment, and a long list of everyday items down to cheese and toilet paper — effective September 8, after the U.S. imposed 50% tariffs on Canadian goods over the weekend and trade talks broke down (CNBC, NPR, Washington Post). It's the sharpest escalation yet in a trade dispute that's been simmering for a year. And yet U.S. stocks rose right through it: the S&P 500 gained 0.3% to 7,677 and the Nasdaq climbed 0.7%, with reporting attributing the calm to investors looking past the tariff headlines toward two bigger catalysts this week — Nvidia's earnings after tonight's close and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech Friday (Yahoo Finance).

Does the state file back up "markets are looking past this"? So far, yes, on every channel that would show stress first. Long rates eased rather than jumped (10Y −6bp to 4.64%, 30Y −6bp to 5.17%) — a tariff shock that truly worried the market about growth or inflation would more typically push yields up, not pull them down. Junk-bond spreads — HY OAS, the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated companies, and the fastest tell for credit-market nerves — barely moved. And breadth, meaning the average stock rather than just the handful of giants, is unusually strong versus its own recent history (today's chart). Tariffs are treated here strictly as a mechanical margin and pass-through question for the named sectors, never as a verdict on the policy itself. If credit or breadth roll over as the September 8 tariff date approaches, this brief will say so — today, they haven't.

## Concept of the day — Unit 1 · segment 6

**Convexity — why duration is a good estimate, not an exact one.** Yesterday's concept was duration — the ruler that predicts how much a bond's price moves for a given yield change. Today's twist: that ruler bends. Duration is really the slope of the price-vs-yield relationship at today's yield — a straight-line approximation of something actually curved. As yields fall, prices rise a little faster than duration predicts; as yields rise, prices fall a little slower. That curvature is convexity — a genuine advantage to the bondholder (gains get a boost, losses get a cushion), part of why highly convex bonds trade at a premium (lower yield) to begin with. It barely shows up on a quiet day like today, when 10Y/30Y both eased a modest 6bp; duration alone explains almost all of a move that small. It matters most on the big moves — the kind this month's 30Y selloff produced, and the kind tonight's Nvidia print or Friday's Jackson Hole speech could still produce.

**Client translation:** "Your bond fund doesn't gain or lose in a perfectly straight line as rates move — it's built with a little extra cushion on the downside and a little extra kick on the upside, which is one more reason a long-term bond fund isn't quite as scary as a simple 'how much will rates hurt me' number makes it sound."
