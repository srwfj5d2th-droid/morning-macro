## Concept of the day — Unit 4, Liquidity plumbing, segment 6

**The quarter-end turn, verified — how fast window dressing actually unwinds.** Yesterday's segment called the ON RRP facility's $11.54 billion two-day spike a balance-sheet costume change, not a liquidity event — banks temporarily shrinking their repo-lending books to look smaller on a regulatory snapshot date, with the prediction that it would unwind within days once the new quarter's reporting date had passed. Today's own number (Dashboard, above) didn't wait days: ON RRP fell from $11.54 billion back to $0.35 billion in a single session, essentially all the way back to where it sat before the spike. That speed is itself the useful information. A real liquidity problem — genuinely scarce bank reserves, or dealers unable to find cash — doesn't resolve in one day just because the calendar flipped; it takes real money moving, which takes time. A regulatory snapshot effect, by contrast, is mechanically tied to one date on the calendar, so once that date passes, the behavior that caused it has no reason to continue, and it unwinds immediately. The speed of an unwind is a diagnostic in its own right: slow-motion reversals look like real stress working itself out, and one-day round-trips look like exactly what this one was.

**Client translation:** "That overnight-cash spike at the Fed wasn't new money piling in on the sidelines — it was banks tidying their books for one regulatory snapshot date, and the fact that it reversed in a single day is itself the proof."
