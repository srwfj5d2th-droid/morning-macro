Concept of the day · Unit 2 · Real vs. nominal decomposition · segment 2 of roughly 6

Reading which piece moved: Friday's hawkish repricing, decomposed

Yesterday's segment established the identity: nominal 10-year yield ≈ real yield (measured by TIPS, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) + breakeven inflation (the market's implied average inflation rate). Today, put it to work on an actual event. Friday's hawkish Jackson Hole speech pushed the nominal 10-year yield up 6 basis points, from 4.67% Thursday to 4.73% Friday (the most recent day with both pieces published). Split it: the real yield — roughly what investors demand to lend for ten years after inflation, mostly a growth-and-Fed-policy number — rose 8 basis points, from 2.34% to 2.42%. The breakeven — the market's implied inflation rate — actually fell 2 basis points, from 2.33% to 2.31%. Add the two moves together (+8bp and −2bp) and you get +6bp, matching the nominal move to the basis point. That's the tell: Friday's yield spike was almost entirely a real-yield story, not an inflation story. In plain terms, the market didn't get more worried about inflation coming back — it got more convinced the Fed will keep policy tight for longer, which is exactly what a hawkish speech should produce. A move driven by breakevens instead would point to a very different, inflation-scare narrative, with different implications for TIPS, commodities, and long-duration bonds.

Client translation: "When yields jump the way they did Friday, the first question I ask is whether it's the inflation piece or the real, after-inflation piece that moved — Friday it was almost entirely the real piece, which tells us this was a 'the Fed is staying tougher for longer' move, not a fresh inflation scare."
