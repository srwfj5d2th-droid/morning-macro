## The story

The press narrative this morning is squarely about the Fed. CNBC and Bloomberg both lead with Thursday's remarks from Fed Governor Christopher Waller, who said he would be "inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its current setting" at the September 15-16 meeting, provided upcoming inflation data — chiefly the August CPI report, due before that meeting — keeps showing progress toward the Fed's 2% goal. He left the door open the other way too. The market-reaction numbers vary slightly by source: CNBC cites CME FedWatch odds of a September hike falling from 63.2% to 50.4%, while a separate Yahoo Finance/Bloomberg-sourced report puts the move at roughly 12 points to about 54.6% — both describe the same directional shift on one governor's conditional comments.

**Does the state file confirm the dovish-turn story? Partially, and the size of the market reaction outran the size of the actual rate move.** The mechanical facts: the 10-year fell 2bp to 4.77%, the 2-year fell 5bp to 4.34%, the 30-year fell 2bp to 5.25% — real, same-direction moves, but small ones. What moved a lot more: the S&P 500 (+1.06%), the Nasdaq (+1.40%), gold (+2.87%, this system's largest one-day gold move on record so far), and the dollar (−0.56%) — the pattern you'd expect from a dovish repricing, but usually reserved for bigger rate moves than 2-5bp. The complication: every tenor on the curve is still flagged (§4, |z| ≥ 1.5 — among the more unusual readings of the past six months), meaning Thursday eased the edges of a still-elevated curve rather than reversing it. This reads less like "the Fed just turned dovish" and more like a stretched market taking a small excuse to unwind some of that stretch — a real move, not yet the size the headlines imply. This morning's payrolls report is the next, more direct test (see Calendar and CL-0010).

## Concept of the day

**Unit 2 · Real vs. nominal decomposition · segment 5 of roughly 6 — Where a Fed comment shows up first: routing today's move through the four-piece decomposition**

Segments 3 and 4 split the 10-year yield into four pieces: an expected-real-rate-path piece and a real-term-premium piece (inside the TIPS real yield), and an expected-inflation piece and an inflation-risk-premium piece (inside the breakeven). Today's data is a clean, live example of why that split is useful: Waller made a comment about the future path of the fed funds rate, and the piece of the curve built specifically to price the expected policy path — the front end — is exactly where that comment landed first. The 2-year, the most policy-sensitive point, moved the most (−5bp); the 10-year, part policy-path and part term premium, moved less (−2bp); the lagged real-yield and breakeven readings (still dated Wednesday) hadn't caught up to any of it. That's the practical use of the framework: news specifically about the Fed's rate path should hit the front end and the real-rate-path component first and hardest, with breakevens — the inflation-expectations piece — much slower to react, since a comment about holding rates isn't, by itself, new information about future inflation.

**Client translation:** "When a Fed official talks about pausing rate hikes, watch the short end of the bond market first — that's the part built to react to exactly that kind of news, while the long end and the inflation-expectations piece usually take longer to move, if they move at all."
