Financing watch — weekly deep dive (§5 Tier 3, Friday): this week's clearest financing-cycle entry is Oracle's force-majeure notice on Project Jupiter, its $165 billion flagship AI data center in New Mexico. Per Bloomberg, TechCrunch, and CNBC: Oracle sent the project's developer — a unit of Blue Owl Capital — a notice citing force majeure (a contract clause that can pause or reallocate financial obligations when an outside event, here reported as power-supply delays, prevents a party from performing) rather than attempting to exit the project outright. A spokesperson said "Project Jupiter remains on our planned schedule"; Blue Owl disputed that the notice changes the deal's financial commitments. Read plainly: the party actually leasing and using the data center is trying to shift some of the financial risk of a delay onto its counterparty, over a real-world bottleneck (power availability) that this system has not previously logged for the AI buildout. That is exactly the kind of financing-structure novelty Unit 7 (not yet reached in this curriculum) will formally classify against the Lucent reference class — a cycle-level observation, not a prediction, and not a view on Oracle, Blue Owl, or any NR-relevant name (§8). Separately, Akamai fell 6.78% today despite announcing an $11.6 billion, multi-year cloud-infrastructure deal with Anthropic (per a research note picked up by Cryptonomist and Yahoo Finance) — UBS and TD Cowen raised price targets on the deal itself, but reporting says investors are weighing roughly $5.5 billion of related capital spending and near-term profit pressure against broader Treasury-yield jitters (Movers, above). Both stories are the same underlying tension in miniature: real, large AI-infrastructure demand, arriving with real financing and capital-spending strain attached. That tension shows up at the aggregate level too — research this system has seen cited (Sage Advisory, via Janus Henderson and other outlets) puts AI-related corporate bond spreads meaningfully wider than the broad investment-grade market (roughly 115bp vs. roughly 78bp), even as this system's own aggregate IG OAS reading (Dashboard, above) stays flat. A market getting choosier about AI-specific credit while staying calm on credit broadly is one of the earliest, most gradual forms financing-cycle strain can take, long before it shows up in an aggregate index — logged as a fact worth tracking, not a signal either way.

Weekly liquidity panel

This week's Thursday H.4.1 release landed in today's pull (dated Wednesday 09-23): the Fed's balance sheet was essentially unchanged week-over-week, up $1.156 billion to $6.7477 trillion — QT running at a slow, steady pace, not accelerating. The Treasury General Account (TGA, the government's own checking account at the Fed) jumped $100.056 billion in a week, to $977.08 billion — a large one-week cash build, consistent with this week's Treasury note settlements (the same $70 billion five-year auction behind CL-0022, above) landing in the government's account before it's spent back out. Money sitting in TGA is money pulled out of the banking system; money spent from TGA pushes it back in — today's concept of the day (below) walks through why that matters. The overnight reverse repo facility (ON RRP, a Fed-run parking spot where money-market funds can safely earn interest on excess cash) ticked up to $0.63 billion, still near the bottom of its multi-year drain (down over 99% from where this system's tracking began) — there's very little left in that buffer to cushion swings like this week's TGA build.

Concept of the day · Unit 4 · Liquidity plumbing · segment 1

Where bank reserves actually come from — and why a $100 billion Treasury cash build is a plumbing fact, not a mystery

Unit 3 closed yesterday on the question "does credit confirm the rate story?" Unit 4 opens on a different question: where does the cash in the banking system actually come from and go? Three accounts do the work. The Fed's balance sheet (currently $6.75 trillion) is the total pool of reserves — cash sitting at the Fed that banks use to settle payments with each other; it shrinks slowly and mechanically as the Fed lets bonds mature without replacing them ("QT," quantitative tightening). The Treasury General Account, or TGA, is the federal government's own checking account, also held at the Fed — when the government sells new debt (this week's $70 billion five-year note auction, CL-0022 above) or collects taxes, cash moves out of the banking system and into TGA; when the government spends it, cash moves back out into the system. The overnight reverse repo facility, ON RRP, used to be a shock absorber: money-market funds parked excess cash there instead of the banking system, so a TGA build didn't squeeze bank reserves directly. That buffer is now almost empty (Weekly liquidity panel, above) — so a $100 billion TGA build in one week, like this week's, increasingly shows up as a direct, if usually temporary, reduction in bank reserves rather than being cushioned. None of this is a forecast of stress; it's simply how the plumbing works, and it's the mechanism this system will use for the rest of the unit to trace a Treasury refunding decision through to what a bank, and eventually a client's money-market fund, actually feels.

Client translation: "When the Treasury sells a big batch of new bonds, like it did this week, that cash sits in the government's own account for a bit before it's spent back out — it's a timing mechanic, not a sign of a cash crunch, and it's one of the quieter reasons short-term borrowing costs have stayed firm even though nothing about the economy itself changed this week."
