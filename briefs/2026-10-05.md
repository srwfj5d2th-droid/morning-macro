## Concept of the day — Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, segment 7

**Settlement day, not auction day — when a Treasury auction actually drains reserves.** Segment 3 gave the reserves identity: bank reserves ≈ the Fed's balance sheet minus the Treasury's checking account (the TGA) minus the overnight-parking facility (ON RRP). An auction announcement doesn't move that identity by itself — what moves it is settlement, the day the Treasury actually collects cash from the winning bidders, which usually lands one or two business days after the auction itself, not on it. Until settlement, the TGA hasn't actually grown; the government has promised to receive money, but hasn't received it yet. This week gives a live test: Wednesday's 10-year and Thursday's 30-year auctions (Calendar, above) are each followed by a settlement date a session or two later — that's when to watch the TGA line in Friday's weekly H.4.1 release, not the auction days themselves. It's also the opposite case from segment 6's quarter-end spike: that was a one-day regulatory snapshot effect that reversed itself immediately once the date passed. A settlement-driven TGA build is the government actually holding more cash, pulled out of the banking system's reserves — it only reverses once the Treasury spends that cash back out, which takes real time, not a single session.

**Client translation:** "When you hear the Treasury had a big bond auction this week, the thing to watch isn't auction day itself — it's a day or two later, once the government actually collects the cash, that you'd see any tightening ripple through day-to-day funding markets."
