Concept of the day · Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, segment 2

Why a scarce-reserves squeeze shows up in SOFR first — and how today's reading tells you it isn't one

Segment 1 introduced three pieces of the Fed's plumbing: bank reserves (cash banks keep parked at the Fed), the Treasury General Account or TGA (the government's own checking account, also at the Fed), and the overnight reverse repo facility or ON RRP (a parking spot for spare cash, now drained to a sliver of what it once held). When the TGA builds — as it did by $100 billion this month — that cash comes out of the banking system's reserves. With the ON RRP buffer nearly empty, there's very little cushion left to absorb a move like that. If reserves actually got scarce relative to demand for them, the first place it would show up is SOFR (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate — the rate banks charge each other to borrow cash overnight, and the base rate underneath most floating-rate loans) creeping toward or through the top of the Fed's target range, even with no Fed hike behind it. That's the genuine stress signal to watch for. Today's reading is the useful contrast: CL-0020, confirmed this morning, shows SOFR's jump was a clean level shift tied directly to an actual Fed hike three weeks ago, sitting calmly inside the new range the whole time — not reserves running thin. Same gauge, two very different stories; the trick is knowing which one you're looking at before reacting to either.

Client translation: "Short-term borrowing costs are already up because the Fed just hiked — the thing I'm actually watching for is whether that rate keeps climbing on its own, with no Fed move behind it, because that would tell us the banking system's spare cash cushion is getting thin, not just that policy got tighter."

Client lens

"The news keeps saying bond yields are at multiyear highs — is my bond fund still going to lose money?" A fair question given this week's headlines. Worth separating two things for a client: existing bond funds do take a mark-to-market hit when yields rise (prices move opposite yields), which is exactly what's been happening across this system's dashboard for weeks. But new money going into bonds, CDs, or money-market funds today locks in meaningfully higher income than a year ago — the same rate move that hurts existing holdings pays better going forward. Both things are true at once.

"Did you see Dell and Bloom Energy jump today — should I be buying AI stocks?" Expected staple this week given the size of both moves (Movers, above). Answered from the read-throughs, not a recommendation: both moves reflect real, continuing demand for AI infrastructure — compute (Dell's backlog) and power (Bloom Energy's data-center deals) — but Tier 3's financing-watch note (above) is the other half of that same story: the debt funding this buildout is pricier and more closely scrutinized than a year ago. Strong demand and rising financing cost are both real at the same time; that's the honest, two-sided answer, not a stock pick.

"Are gas prices going to spike again because of what's happening in the Middle East?" Likely to come up given the weekend's oil-supply headlines (Story, above). This system covers the geopolitics strictly as a transmission mechanism, never as a policy view (§4D): if the oil move holds (CL-0024 is testing exactly that over the next few sessions), it's a real input-cost pressure worth naming plainly — pump prices and, at the margin, inflation — without asserting yet whether it's a durable shift or a headline that fades, which this system doesn't know until the data says so.
