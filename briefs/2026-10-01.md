## Concept of the day — Unit 4, Liquidity plumbing, segment 5

**The quarter-end turn — why ON RRP usage jumps even in a "drained" facility, once a quarter.** Segment 4 called the ON RRP facility a nearly-drained shock absorber — usage had fallen to a few hundred million dollars for weeks. Today's own number (Dashboard, above) jumped to $11.54 billion in two sessions. That is not new cash flooding into the system; it is the quarter-end turn, a well-documented, recurring event that happens every March, June, September, and December 31st (or the last business day before it). Banks report their balance sheets to regulators at quarter-end, and one of the ratios they're graded on (the supplementary leverage ratio) counts total assets, repo loans included, regardless of how safe those loans are. So for a few days around quarter-end, banks and bond dealers temporarily shrink their repo-lending books to look smaller and better-capitalized on that one snapshot date — even though nothing about their actual risk changed. Money-market funds that would normally lend that cash to dealers overnight suddenly have fewer willing borrowers, so they default to lending it to the one counterparty that always takes it: the Fed's own ON RRP facility. It is a balance-sheet costume change, not a liquidity event, and it reliably unwinds within a day or two once the new quarter's reporting date has passed — worth knowing so a future spike doesn't get mistaken for a repo-market problem.

**Client translation:** "The giant one-day jump in cash parked at the Fed isn't new money sitting on the sidelines — it's banks temporarily tidying up their books for a quarter-end report card, and it normally unwinds within a few days."
