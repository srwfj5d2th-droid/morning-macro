## The story

**The press blamed last week on AI worries and high oil prices; Reuters tied the AI part to debt.** The Associated Press reported that the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both had their worst week since June, and that chip companies “pulled back some more as worries deepened about how long the frenzy over the AI buildout will last.” Reuters’ closing headline read “Wall Street ends down sharply; worries about AI trade and high oil prices weigh.” Reuters tied the AI selloff to concerns that “debt-laden AI infrastructure companies may not turn profits in time to repay debts, particularly with borrowing costs expected to rise.” CNBC’s close: “Nasdaq slides 2% Friday to post worst week since June, pressured by AI sell-off and oil prices.” The Wall Street Journal’s and Financial Times’ own pages couldn’t be reached from this system’s search, so their framing isn’t reported.

**What the data confirms, and what it complicates.** The size checks out. The S&P 500 fell 1.09% Friday and 1.95% for the week; the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.15% and 4.48%. In this system’s own closes, both are the worst weeks since the one that ended June 26. The chip fund SMH fell 3.41% Friday and 10.53% for the week. The “borrowing costs” part needs care. Treasury yields fell: the 2-year 3bp Friday, the 10-year 15bp for the week. Junk bonds are another matter. The broad junk-bond spread (dated Thursday) is 3.23%, up from 3.11% on Oct 9, and only two readings in the past six months were higher. So the higher borrowing costs in the press accounts are not in Treasuries. They are sharpest in specific AI builders’ debt (Tier 3) and show up more mildly in the broad junk index. Friday’s credit print arrives on tomorrow’s run (CL-0048).

This brief takes no side on any of these policies; it tracks what they do to supply and prices.

## Concept of the day — Unit 6 — Positioning & sentiment vs. fundamentals, segment 3

**Good news, falling price: “priced in” vs. “improving”.** Unit 6’s goal is telling “priced in” apart from “improving.” Last week gave a clean example. On Thursday, TSMC, the main contract chipmaker for AI processors, reported third-quarter net income up 68.7% from a year earlier (company filing, Oct 15), above the LSEG consensus cited in pre-results coverage. The business is improving. Yet the chip fund SMH fell 1.04% that day, 3.41% on Friday and 10.53% for the week, its ninth straight decline. How can both be true? A stock price is a bet on the future, and it already contains what investors expected. When expectations are very high, good news has to beat them by a lot just to hold the price. Last week’s coverage also focused on a different question: whether the customers buying these chips can keep paying for the build-out (Tier 3). One reading, the one the press offered, is that the price was answering that question, not the earnings report. Improving means the business is getting better. Priced in means the price already assumed it would. Surprises come from the gap between the two.

**Client translation:** "A company can report great results and its stock can still fall, because the price already assumed great results; what moves the price is how the news compares with what investors expected."
