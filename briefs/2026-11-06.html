The story

Thursday was the kind of session financial media exists to describe. Yahoo Finance's market blog ran “Dow plunges 1,800 points, Nasdaq enters correction in AI trade meltdown”; CNN called it a tech sell-off that wiped roughly $2.5 trillion in market value; Fortune framed it as the day “Wall Street's best post-midterm rally in 44 years lasted just one day” before $3 trillion vanished; and Investing.com's Reuters feed tied the move to this week's midterm election results — read as raising the odds of new restrictions on permitting AI data centers — plus renewed worry about a December rate decision, cited at over 65% odds. All four outlets agree on the category of trigger (politics and rates); none claims to know which one did more of the work, and neither does this brief.

This system's own pull backs the headline numbers almost exactly, and adds the piece the headlines leave out. The S&P 500 closed down 4.36% (matching the roughly 4.4% cited above), and SMH (the chip-sector ETF this brief tracks as an AI-capex proxy) fell 11.2% to $447.73 — today's largest flag by a wide margin (z −3.90, against +2.44 on IG OAS and +2.18 on HY OAS, the next-largest). What the press framing leaves out is credit's reaction, which is the test this brief's own epistemics rules (§4C) require before accepting a causal story: HY OAS and IG OAS — already flagged on Tuesday's lagged print — stayed in their own widened range rather than snapping back, the clearest same-day confirmation credit has offered an equity selloff this run. Treasury yields eased across the curve in the kind of flight-to-quality move that tends to accompany a credit-confirmed selloff, not a sentiment-only one — with one exception: the dollar (DXY) barely moved, +0.04%, the one major asset class that didn't visibly react. Three of four confirm; one shrugs.

AppLovin was the one clean exception on the equity side, and it complicates the simplest version of today's story. It posted Q3 results Tuesday night (EPS $4.30 vs. roughly $4.01–$4.03 per Benzinga's tracked consensus; revenue about $2.19B vs. its own $2.06–$2.08B guided range, per the company's SEC 8-K exhibit) and rallied 31.04% the next session — a reminder that today's selloff reads as a financing/capex-risk repricing, not a blanket verdict on AI-linked demand everywhere (Movers, below).

Treasury yields eased across the whole curve Thursday, in the kind of broad flight-to-quality move that typically shows up the same day stocks sell off hard. The 3-month bill fell 4bp to 4.17% (z +1.17, not flagged; 52nd percentile since 1981 and the 70th of the last 30 years — no real gap, cite either). The 2-year fell 12bp to 4.46% (z +0.35; 48th/77th percentile, † — a real gap, cite both). The 10-year fell 6bp to 4.84% (z +0.32; 45th/81st, †) and the 30-year fell 3bp to 5.25% (z +0.21; 46th/80th, † — today's lead History-check fact, below). Why this matters: when yields fall this broadly without the Fed doing anything, it's the bond market pricing in a little more growth caution, not a rate cut — the kind of move that nudges a new CD or money-market quote a hair lower at the margin without doing much yet to the rate on a new 30-year home loan.

The curve itself moved only modestly and in slightly different directions at each end: 2s10s widened 6bp to 38bp while 3m10y tightened 2bp to 67bp — both still comfortably positive, far from the inversion that shows up on this morning's longer-run watch-list (History, below).

Credit, by contrast, didn't ease at all, and that's the day's real signal. HY OAS (the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated companies, dated Wednesday 11-04, the usual one-day lag) held at 3.32% (z +2.18, flagged; 80th percentile of its own ~3-year record, §4F, marked *). IG OAS, the investment-grade equivalent (also dated 11-04), held at 0.91% (z +2.44, flagged, today's largest Tier 1 flag; 66th percentile of its own ~3-year record, also *). SOFR, the overnight bank-to-bank borrowing rate (also dated 11-04), held at 3.94% (z +1.91, flagged; 45th percentile since 1954 but the 68th of the last 30 years, †). Why this matters: a credit spread that stays wide on a day stocks fall hard is credit agreeing with the selloff rather than shrugging it off — lenders, who have real money at risk, aren't getting more comfortable, which is a different and more durable kind of worry than a one-day stock-price wobble.

The dollar (DXY) was the one asset that didn't seem to notice: it rose a bare 0.04% to 101.55 (z +0.97, not flagged; 70th/79th percentile, no real gap). Fed balance-sheet and TGA readings are still this week's Thursday H.4.1/DTS release, carried forward unchanged — thin history, no pre-2008 comparison exists for either series (§4F). ON RRP, the nearly drained overnight-cash shock absorber, eased to $0.13B (z −0.54, not flagged). Why this matters: on a day equities, credit, and the bond market all moved together, a flat dollar is the odd one out — not every asset class has to confirm a story for the story to be real, but it's worth naming which one didn't.

Concept of the day · Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 2

Agreement is the strongest signal — when every asset class moves the same way, there's no tie to break

Segment 1 built a hierarchy for when asset classes disagree: weight credit over equity sentiment, and a single name over neither. Today hands the unit the opposite case, which matters just as much. The S&P 500 fell 4.36%, credit (HY OAS and IG OAS, Dashboard above) stayed in its own widened, flagged range rather than snapping back, and Treasury yields eased in a flight-to-quality move — three different corners of the market, three different kinds of participants (stock traders, bond buyers, and credit lenders), all moving the same direction the same day. When that happens, segment 1's hierarchy doesn't need to break a tie, because there isn't one: agreement across asset classes that don't have to agree is itself stronger evidence than any single market's move alone. The one asset that didn't confirm — the dollar, essentially flat at +0.04% — is worth naming precisely because it's the exception: a real signal doesn't require every market to move, but it's worth tracking whether the dollar's lack of reaction holds up or catches down over the next few sessions.

Client translation: When stocks, bonds, and the credit market all move the same direction on the same day, that's a stronger signal than any one of them moving alone -- it's the market equivalent of three different witnesses telling the same story.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
