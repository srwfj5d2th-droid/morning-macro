The story

The circulating narrative this morning is a wall of beat-and-raise earnings. Salesforce reported fiscal Q2 revenue up 11% to $11.35 billion with adjusted earnings per share of $5.90 against a Street estimate near $3.28, then lifted its fiscal 2027 adjusted EPS guidance to $16.67-$16.71 versus the roughly $14.16 Wall Street had modeled -- and paired it with a new partnership with Anthropic to build AI agent tools into its products (Motley Fool, Gurufocus). The stock closed up 22.58%, from $205.62 to $252.05. CrowdStrike's fiscal Q2 revenue rose 26% year-over-year to $1.47 billion, beat the $1.44 billion estimate, and the company raised full-year and long-term revenue guidance, with its CEO calling it the best quarter in the company's history (CNBC, StockStory); the stock rose 20.50%, from $189.18 to $227.96. And Nvidia -- whose print landed after Wednesday's close -- posted $96.2 billion in quarterly revenue (up 106% year-over-year) with data-center revenue (server-chip sales to cloud and AI companies) of $89 billion beating the roughly $86.3 billion consensus, then guided next quarter to $108 billion, above the ~$104.2 billion Street number (CNBC); the stock added another 8.74% yesterday on top of the after-hours pop. Gurufocus's framing this morning ties the two headline beats together directly: "Salesforce and Nvidia earnings defy market expectations."

Does the state file confirm this? Yes, and unusually cleanly for a story this large. The S&P 500 rose 0.72% to 7,730.99, now just 0.87% off its 08-13 closing high -- but that index-level number understates what actually happened, because the gain was concentrated: the Technology sector ETF (XLK) beat the S&P by 2.50 percentage points on the day, the largest sector divergence on the board, while five other sectors (Consumer Staples, Health Care, Consumer Discretionary, Communication Services, Real Estate) each lagged by more than 1.5 points. That's a market being pulled higher by a specific earnings cluster, not broad-based buying -- and it shows up directly in today's chart of the day (below): RSP, the equal-weight version of the S&P that treats every stock the same regardless of size, actually fell 0.30% yesterday even as the cap-weighted index rallied, and RSP's level is still the single largest flag on the whole board (z +1.56) purely because of how strong the broader market has been for months, not because of yesterday specifically. Underneath all of it, credit stayed calm rather than getting nervous about how much of this rally rides on a few names: high-yield spreads (HY OAS -- the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated companies over safe Treasurys, a real-time stress gauge) tightened another 3 basis points to 2.67% (z -0.91, comfortably below-normal, i.e. calm), which is what "confirming" means in today's regime tag -- equities up, credit agreeing there's no reason to worry.

The Tier 1 spine had another quiet day, and it was quiet in an interesting, specific way: the 2-year, 10-year, and 30-year Treasury yields all rose by exactly 1 basis point (a hundredth of a percentage point) to 4.20%, 4.67%, and 5.19% respectively -- the same uniform, parallel move this brief flagged yesterday at 2bp, just smaller. As Wednesday's curriculum segment covered, a move that shows up roughly evenly across the whole curve, rather than concentrated at the front end where Fed-meeting expectations live, reads as a small term-premium touch (investors asking a hair more compensation for tying up money long-term) rather than a repricing of what the Fed does next -- today's concept segment builds the full toolkit for telling these apart. None of it is large enough to matter: every Tier 1 series stayed inside its normal 6-month range (largest z-score is the 30-year at +1.36, short of the 1.5 flag line). High-yield credit spreads -- the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated companies, and this brief's real-time stress gauge -- tightened 3bp to 2.67% (z -0.91), which matters for anyone in a high-yield bond fund or a leveraged-loan allocation: it's a sign lenders aren't demanding more protection despite a market rally concentrated in a handful of stocks. Concept of the day · Unit 1 · Yield curve mechanics · segment 8 of 8 (capstone)

Putting it together — reading any curve move in three questions

Seven segments have built separate tools: naming moves (steepener/flattener), duration, convexity, and the path-versus-premium split. The capstone is one habit that uses all of them on any curve move, in order. First: which part of the curve moved -- just the front end (2-year and in), just the long end (10-year and out), or all of it? Second: how big, relative to duration -- the same yield-basis-point move swings a 30-year bond's price harder than a 2-year's, so a uniform bp move across maturities isn't a uniform-size event in return terms. Third, and the payoff of segment 7: what's left after subtracting the expected path -- a front-end-only move is usually a Fed-expectations repricing; a move spread evenly across the whole curve, like today's, is usually a small term-premium touch instead. Run today's data through it: the 2-year, 10-year, and 30-year all rose the same 1 basis point yesterday -- question one says "all of it, evenly"; question three says that pattern points to premium, not path. The honest answer the toolkit gives is "not much happened" -- which is exactly the right answer, and exactly the skill this system needs working correctly a few hours before a Fed chair speaks.

Client translation: "There's a repeatable way to tell whether a Treasury-yield move is the market repricing what the Fed does next or just investors wanting a little more compensation for lending long-term -- and today's move, small and even across every maturity, is the second kind, not a signal to change anything."
