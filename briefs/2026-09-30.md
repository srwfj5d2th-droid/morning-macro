## Concept of the day — Unit 4, Liquidity plumbing, segment 4

**QT versus the TGA drain — two ways to shrink reserves that look identical in the reserves identity but mean different things.** Segment 3's identity said reserves ≈ the Fed's balance sheet minus the TGA minus the ON RRP — and showed a big TGA build draining reserves without the Fed doing anything. But there's a second way reserves shrink: the Fed's own balance-sheet runoff, often called quantitative tightening (QT), where the Fed lets bonds mature without buying new ones to replace them. Both show up as the same falling number in the identity; they are not the same event. A TGA-driven drain is temporary and mechanical — the cash sits in the government's account until it's spent back out, and reserves refill. A QT-driven drain is the Fed's own deliberate, ongoing policy choice, and it doesn't refill on its own. This week's own numbers (all thin history) show which one is actually happening right now: the Fed's balance sheet itself barely moved, +$1.156 billion week over week, while the TGA alone moved nearly a hundred times that, +$100.056 billion. This week's reserve pressure is almost entirely the Treasury's doing, not the Fed actively shrinking its balance sheet — a distinction that matters because one of these reverses itself in weeks and the other doesn't reverse until the Fed decides to stop it.

**Client translation:** "When the news says the Fed is 'still doing QT,' that's a separate, slower story from a big Treasury bond sale temporarily soaking up cash from the banking system — and this week, the numbers say it's almost entirely the second one, which tends to ease up on its own once the government spends that cash back out."
