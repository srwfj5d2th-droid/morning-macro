The story

Friday's coverage (CNBC, Benzinga) led with a reversal in chip stocks: AMD fell 8.75% to $534.06, Dell 7.18% to $344.53, and Cisco 6.24% to $95.64, even though Cisco had beaten its first-quarter estimates and raised full-year guidance that same week — reporting attributed the drop to profit-taking and margin concerns rather than any demand-side news. Benzinga's wrap tied the move to the broader chip complex giving back the week's sharp rally; the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell roughly 3.4% and Nvidia extended a losing streak heading into its own earnings Tuesday after the close. No distinct, dated catalyst for Dell specifically surfaced in today's search — worth saying plainly rather than guessing at one.

This system's own pull backs the headline move but complicates the "AI trade in doubt" framing those headlines imply. All three names gave back more than just Friday: this system's own closes show Dell and Cisco each ended the week lower than where they started it (Dell $344.53 vs. Monday's $346.31; Cisco $95.64 vs. Monday's $98.65), while AMD still closed the week up 5.0% from Monday despite Friday's drop — a partial round-trip, not a one-day shock, after all three rallied hard Monday through Thursday. And the damage stayed narrow: the S&P 500 fell just 0.36% and the equal-weight average (RSP) actually rose 0.20% (Unit 6's breadth tool), while Technology (XLK) underperformed the index by 1.65 percentage points and Consumer Staples (XLP) outperformed by 1.78 — money rotating out of chips and into defensives, not a broad selloff. Credit didn't confirm stress either: HY OAS eased below its flag line (z +1.38) even as IG OAS stayed flagged (z +1.70), a mixed, not alarming, credit read.

The move this system's own data flags loudest, though, sat outside Friday's headlines entirely: the dollar. DXY has climbed every session this week, now at its highest level in six months (chart of the day, below) — and copper, priced in dollars worldwide, fell to this run's second-largest flag (z −2.07) over the same stretch. That's the dollar-transmission channel Unit 5 covered, showing up live. Today's calendar carries a scheduled, high-impact speech from Fed Chair Kevin Warsh at noon ET — a known event, not a surprise, so this morning frames what to watch rather than guessing the outcome (new claim CL-0068, Flags, below).

Every Treasury tenor rose this week, but the move was front-end-led. The 3-month bill is flat on the day but up 15bp over five sessions to 4.32% (z +1.85, flagged; 53rd percentile since 1977 but the 72nd of the last 30 years — an 18-point gap, not quite the 20-point bar that forces dual citation, but close). The 2-year — the part of the curve that runs as a live poll of Fed expectations — rose 2bp to 4.88% and 42bp over five sessions (z +1.77, flagged; 54th percentile since 1976 but the 84th of the last 30 years, † a 30-point gap). The 10-year rose 5bp to 5.15% (z +1.45, just under the flag line; 48th/86th, † — today's lead History-check fact, below, where that same gap is the whole story) and the 30-year rose 4bp to 5.47% (z +1.26; 48th/84th, †). Why this matters: a front-end-led rise like this is mostly a repricing of how many more times the Fed moves, not a growth or inflation story for the next decade — closer to what sets a new CD rate than a 30-year mortgage quote, though five straight sessions of this did eventually touch the long end too.

The curve's two slices disagreed on direction this week: 2s10s flattened 11bp to 27bp (29th/31st percentile, no real gap, nowhere near inversion) while 3m10y widened 16bp to 83bp (31st/38th) — the long end and the very front moved more than the belly did, a lopsided version of this week's front-end-led story.

The 10-year TIPS real yield — what Treasuries pay after inflation, dated Thursday — rose 8bp to 2.86% (z +1.65, flagged), while the 10-year breakeven — the market's own inflation forecast baked into nominal yields — rose 4bp to 2.24% (z −1.14, not flagged). Nearly all of this week's 10-year move was a real-rate story, not an inflation story (Unit 2). Why this matters: a real-rate-led rise raises the actual cost of borrowing after accounting for inflation — the kind of move that eventually shows up in variable-rate loans and new mortgage quotes, not just Fed-futures pricing.

Credit split in opposite directions this week, which is the one piece of good news in an otherwise tightening tape. HY OAS (dated Thursday, the usual one-day lag) eased 10bp to 3.20% (z +1.38, cleared its own flag line; 70th percentile of its own ~3-year record, §4F, marked *). IG OAS (also Thursday) eased just 1bp to 0.89% (z +1.70, still flagged; 61st percentile of its own ~3-year record, also *). Why this matters: lenders to junk-rated borrowers got slightly less worried this week even as lenders to investment-grade borrowers stayed about as cautious as they were — two different risk gauges, not one, and they're not telling quite the same story right now (Unit 3).

The dollar is this week's loudest single move. DXY rose 24bp Friday and 111bp (1.1%) over five sessions to 103.41 — this run's largest flag (z +2.44; 83rd percentile of the last 30 years, 74th since 1971, no real gap between the two). SOFR (dated Thursday) ticked up 1bp to 3.90% (z +1.45, not flagged; 45th percentile since 1954 but the 67th of the last 30 years, †, a 22-point gap). Why this matters: a dollar this strong raises the local-currency cost of dollar debt for borrowers outside the U.S. and makes dollar-priced commodities more expensive everywhere else — exactly the transmission loop behind this morning's copper and gold weakness (Story, above; Cross-asset, below).

Concept of the day · Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 6

A fourth vote joins the hierarchy — the dollar tends to side with rates, not credit, because both price off the same real-rate input

Segment 5 showed the hierarchy splitting three ways — rates, credit, and breadth each answering a different question. This week adds a fourth voice: the dollar. DXY's climb to this run's largest flag (z +2.44, Chart of the day, above) isn't really a separate opinion from the front-end-led rate rise (Dashboard, above) — both are priced off the same thing, how much the Fed is expected to keep rates elevated, which is why they tend to move together rather than disagree. That's different from credit's job: credit prices default and liquidity risk, a genuinely separate question from the rate path, which is exactly why HY OAS eased this week (z +1.38, cleared its flag) even as rates and the dollar both climbed — not a contradiction, just two different gauges answering two different questions, with the dollar siding with rates rather than with credit because of what it's actually built to measure.

The practical test: when the dollar and rates move together, that's not independent confirmation the way credit agreeing with equities would be — it's closer to the same signal measured twice. Independent confirmation has to come from a gauge pricing a genuinely different risk, which is why credit (and, this week, breadth — RSP's +0.20% day even as the S&P fell) still carry more weight in the hierarchy than a second rates-linked series would.

Client translation: The dollar getting stronger and interest rates rising together this week aren't two separate warning signs — they're basically the same signal (the market expecting the Fed to stay higher for longer) showing up in two places, which is different from credit markets actually getting nervous about defaults, and they haven't.

An account concentrated in that corner of the market had a materially worse week than the index suggests.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
