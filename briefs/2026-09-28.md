## Concept of the day — Unit 4, Liquidity plumbing, segment 2

**Why a scarce-reserves squeeze shows up in SOFR first — and how today's reading tells you it isn't one.** Segment 1 introduced bank reserves, the Treasury General Account (TGA, the government's own checking account at the Fed), and the overnight reverse repo facility (ON RRP, a now-nearly-drained parking spot for spare cash). When the TGA builds — as it did by $100B this month — that cash comes out of banking-system reserves. With ON RRP nearly empty, there's very little cushion left. If reserves actually got scarce relative to demand, the first place it would show up is SOFR (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate — what banks charge each other to borrow cash overnight, the base rate under most floating-rate loans) creeping toward or through the top of the Fed's target range, even with no Fed hike behind it. That's the genuine stress signal to watch for. Today's reading is the useful contrast: CL-0020, confirmed this morning, shows SOFR's jump was a clean level shift tied to an actual Fed hike three weeks ago, sitting calmly inside the new range the whole time — not reserves running thin. Same gauge, two very different stories.

**Client translation:** "Short-term borrowing costs are already up because the Fed just hiked — the thing I'm actually watching for is whether that rate keeps climbing on its own, with no Fed move behind it, because that would tell us the banking system's spare cash cushion is getting thin, not just that policy got tighter."
