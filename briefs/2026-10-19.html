The story

The press called last week an AI-debt scare plus an oil shock. The Associated Press reported that the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both had their worst week since June, and that chip companies “pulled back some more as worries deepened about how long the frenzy over the AI buildout will last.” Reuters’ closing headline read “Wall Street ends down sharply; worries about AI trade and high oil prices weigh.” Reuters tied the AI selloff to concerns that “debt-laden AI infrastructure companies may not turn profits in time to repay debts, particularly with borrowing costs expected to rise.” CNBC’s close: “Nasdaq slides 2% Friday to post worst week since June, pressured by AI sell-off and oil prices.” The Wall Street Journal’s and Financial Times’ own pages couldn’t be reached from this system’s search, so their framing isn’t reported.

What the data confirms, and what it complicates. The size checks out. The S&P 500 fell 1.09% Friday and 1.95% for the week; the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.15% and 4.48%. In this system’s own closes, both are the worst weeks since the one that ended June 26. The chip fund SMH fell 3.41% Friday and 10.53% for the week. But the “borrowing costs” part needs care. Treasury yields fell: the 2-year 3bp Friday, the 10-year 15bp for the week. And the broad junk-bond spread (dated Thursday) slipped 2bp to 3.23%. So the higher borrowing costs in the press accounts show up in specific AI builders’ debt (Tier 3), not in Treasuries or the broad junk index, at least through Thursday’s print. Friday’s credit print, due tonight, tests whether that changed (CL-0048).

The average stock did better than the index again. On Friday the equal-weight RSP fell 0.42% against 1.11% for the cap-weighted SPY. For the week, RSP rose 0.49% while SPY fell 1.97%. The Russell 2000 of smaller companies fell 1.66% to its lowest close since April 16, now 11.31% below its Aug 14 high. Even the 10-year’s drop leaves it high: it has been at or above 5.09% since Sep 23. Before this run, it was last there in July 2007 (Today in history, below).

Oil and the weekend: supply mechanics only. WTI rose 3.51% Friday to $101.41, up 10.41% for the week. That is its first close above $100 since Sept 18 and its highest since Sept 17 (this system’s closes). CNBC led with “Brent oil touches $110 as Iran escalates Hormuz attacks, Ukraine vows to hit Russian refineries.” Reuters, citing the shipping-data firm Kpler, reported that the seven-day average of Gulf crude flows fell to about 8.5 million barrels a day last week, from more than 20 million the week before. Over the weekend, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said two laden tankers caught fire after hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday, and Iranian media reported another on Sunday, per Reuters. None of the incidents has been independently confirmed. Reuters also reported that Iran’s foreign minister said Tehran’s offer to reopen the strait within six days still stands, on conditions that include lifting the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports. AFP and CNBC reported that a Ukrainian drone attack set fires at oil facilities in St. Petersburg, according to the city’s governor. And CNBC and CBS reported that an executive order signed late Friday waived the Graham Act’s new Russia sanctions and tariffs just before they took effect, including tariffs of up to 100% on the largest buyers of Russian crude and gas. As mechanics, that removes a threatened penalty on buyers of Russian oil. Reuters’ Monday oil headline: “Oil slips from 5-month high as Trump’s Russia sanctions waiver may bring more supplies.” This brief takes no side on any of these policies; it tracks what they do to supply and prices.

This morning. CNBC’s Sunday-evening blog led with “Nasdaq futures gain as investors buy the dip in the AI trade.” Reuters reported Monday that stock futures were climbing as AI stocks rebounded, with oil and Treasury yields lower, and that Intel rose about 4% before the open after JPMorgan upgraded it to neutral from underweight. Futures move all morning, so no level is quoted here. Why this matters: last week hit the most popular, most borrowed corner of the market hardest, while bonds rallied and the average stock held up. That is a concentrated selloff, not a broad one, so far.

Concept of the day · Unit 6 — Positioning & sentiment vs. fundamentals, segment 3

Good news, falling price: “priced in” vs. “improving”

Unit 6’s goal is telling “priced in” apart from “improving.” Last week gave a clean example. On Thursday, TSMC, the main contract chipmaker for AI processors, reported third-quarter profit up 68.7% from a year earlier, above the consensus cited in pre-results coverage. The business is improving. Yet the chip fund SMH fell 1.04% that day, 3.41% on Friday and 10.53% for the week, its ninth straight decline. How can both be true? A stock price is a bet on the future, and it already contains what investors expected. When expectations are very high, good news has to beat them by a lot just to hold the price. Last week’s coverage also focused on a different question: whether the customers buying these chips can keep paying for the build-out (Tier 3). So the price was answering a different question from the earnings report. Improving means the business is getting better. Priced in means the price already assumed it would. Surprises come from the gap between the two.

Client translation: A company can report great results and its stock can still fall, because the price already assumed great results; what moves the price is how the news compares with what investors expected.
