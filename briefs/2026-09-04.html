The story

The press narrative this morning is squarely about the Fed. CNBC and Bloomberg both lead with Thursday's remarks from Fed Governor Christopher Waller, who said in a Reuters interview that he would be "inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its current setting" at the September 15-16 meeting, provided upcoming inflation data — chiefly the August CPI report, due before that meeting — keeps showing progress toward the Fed's 2% goal. He left the door open the other way too: a reversal in the inflation data, he said, could still make a case for a hike. The market-reaction numbers vary slightly by source and methodology — CNBC cites CME FedWatch odds of a September hike falling from 63.2% to 50.4%, while a separate Yahoo Finance/Bloomberg-sourced report puts the move at roughly 12 percentage points to about 54.6% — both describe the same directional shift: traders meaningfully pared back hike bets on one Fed governor's conditional comments. PBS News and Investing.com, covering the same remarks, both note how much weight is riding on the word "if."

Does the state file confirm the dovish-turn story? Partially, and the size of the market reaction outran the size of the actual rate move. The mechanical facts: the 10-year Treasury yield fell 2 basis points to 4.77%, the 2-year fell 5bp to 4.34%, and the 30-year fell 2bp to 5.25% — real, same-direction moves, but small ones. What moved a lot more: the S&P 500 (+1.06%), the Nasdaq (+1.40%), gold (+2.87%, the sharpest one-day gold move logged in this system's data), and the dollar (DXY −0.56%) — exactly the pattern you'd expect from a dovish repricing (lower expected rates make gold and stocks more attractive and the dollar less attractive), but a pattern usually reserved for bigger rate moves than 2-5bp. The complication: every tenor on the Treasury curve is still flagged (§4, |z| ≥ 1.5 — among the more unusual readings of the past six months for each series), meaning Thursday's pullback eased the edges of an still-elevated curve rather than reversing it. This reads less like "the Fed just turned dovish" and more like "markets that were already stretched toward a hawkish outcome took a small excuse to unwind some of that stretch" — a real move, but not yet the size of move the headlines imply. This morning's payrolls report is the next, and more direct, test of which reading is right (see Calendar and CL-0010, above).

Concept of the day · Unit 2 · Real vs. nominal decomposition · segment 5 of roughly 6

Where a Fed comment shows up first — routing today's move through the four-piece decomposition

Segments 3 and 4 split the 10-year yield into four pieces: an expected-real-rate-path piece and a real-term-premium piece (inside the TIPS real yield), and an expected-inflation piece and an inflation-risk-premium piece (inside the breakeven). Today's data is a clean, live example of why that split is useful, not just tidy: Fed Governor Waller made a comment about the future path of the fed funds rate, and the piece of the curve that's built specifically to price the expected policy path — the front end, and the real-rate-path component behind it — is exactly where you'd expect that comment to land first. The 2-year yield, the most policy-sensitive point on the curve, moved the most Thursday (−5bp); the 10-year, part policy-path and part term premium, moved less (−2bp); and the lagged real-yield and breakeven readings (still dated Wednesday, one day behind) hadn't caught up to any of it yet. That's the practical use of the four-piece framework: when news is specifically about the Fed's rate path, expect the front end and the real-rate-path component to move first and hardest, with breakevens — the inflation-expectations piece — much slower to react, because a Fed governor's comment about holding rates isn't, by itself, new information about future inflation.

Client translation: "When a Fed official talks about pausing rate hikes, watch the short end of the bond market first — that's the part that's built to react to exactly that kind of news, while the long end and the inflation-expectations piece usually take longer to move, if they move at all."
