## The story

Wednesday's dominant, named-source-attributed story is a split one. On one side, a broad market rally: value, financials, and healthcare names (Bank of America, Mastercard, Merck, Visa, Johnson & Johnson, Goldman Sachs and others all up roughly 3–4%) led the S&P 500 and Nasdaq higher. On the other, a sharp selloff in the AI-power trade: Vistra and NRG, two gas-fired power producers that have become Wall Street's proxy for AI-datacenter electricity demand, each fell more than 13% after Tuesday's Texas gubernatorial election delivered a result read as a threat to new data-center permitting, and KeyBanc analyst Sophie Karp cut Vistra to Sector Weight from Overweight on the resulting policy uncertainty (Yahoo Finance, MarketBeat, GuruFocus). The same election cycle was framed by Fortune as a broader “blue wave,” and Fortune also tied Wednesday's 8.30% crude-oil drop partly to Iran peace-talk optimism arriving the same day as the vote. Separately, and idiosyncratically, FIS (Fidelity National Information Services) fell 23.67% — the S&P 500's largest decliner — after cutting 2026 EPS guidance, flagging a preliminary 2027 EPS decline on client-implementation delays and higher technology/cybersecurity spending, and announcing its CFO's departure, despite an in-line Q3 print (Benzinga, GuruFocus). On the election result itself, this brief has nothing to say about what it means for policy — only how, mechanically, a permitting change would feed through to specific companies' cash flows, which is the question the Vistra/NRG move is actually pricing (§4D).

The state file confirms the rally was genuinely broad, not a cap-weighted illusion: the equal-weight RSP (+1.86%) actually outgained the cap-weighted SPY, the opposite of the narrow-rally pattern this brief's own curriculum has flagged before. It also confirms the AI-power-trade and chip-complex divergence directly — SMH fell 2.75% the same session equities broadly rallied, this run's single sharpest one-day split between the index and that one complex. What it complicates is any read that credit is comfortable with all of this: HY and IG OAS, the premium investors demand to lend to junk- and investment-grade borrowers, barely moved on the day but still carry this morning's two largest statistical flags, sitting in the upper reaches of their own multi-year range (Dashboard, below). ## Concept of the day — Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 1

**A hierarchy-of-evidence habit — what to do when bonds, credit, and equities disagree.** Seven units in, this brief has built tools for reading one asset class at a time: the curve (Unit 1), real vs. nominal yields (Unit 2), credit spreads (Unit 3), liquidity plumbing (Unit 4), the dollar (Unit 5), breadth (Unit 6), and financing cycles (Unit 7). Unit 8 asks a different question: what do you do when they don't agree? The honest answer isn't “pick the one that confirms what you already thought” — it's a rough hierarchy. Credit (Unit 3's lesson) tends to be the most trustworthy tie-breaker, because lenders have money directly at risk and no reason to get swept up in a story; equity moves can be sentiment-driven and reverse fast; a single name's move (like today's FIS) is the least informative of all, since it can simply be that one company's own news. Today handed the unit a clean specimen: equities rallied broadly and the breadth measure actually confirmed it (Unit 6's tool), credit stayed flat but sits in the upper reaches of its own recent range (Dashboard, above) — neither confirming nor denying — and the AI-power trade sold off hard on a political shock that has nothing to do with either. Three readings, one morning: that's the habit this unit exists to build, not resolving the disagreement by force, but naming which asset's signal to weight more and why.

**Client translation:** "When stocks, bonds, and credit are all saying something slightly different on the same morning, the question isn't which one is right -- it's which one has the most skin in the game, and that's usually the lender, not the buyer."
