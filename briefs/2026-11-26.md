## The story

Oracle's five-year credit-default-swap spread — in plain terms, the market price of insuring against Oracle defaulting on its debt — hit a record of roughly 356 basis points overnight, per S&P Global Market Intelligence data reported by BigGo Finance and Webull, more than four times where it traded a year ago; the company's 2031 notes reportedly priced to yield near 18%. That single-name extreme landed next to a broader, milder credit move: HY and IG OAS (the extra yield lenders demand on junk and investment-grade borrowers) both widened today, a reversal from Monday-Tuesday's pattern of chip-sector selling with credit staying calm (Concept, below). The state file confirms the shift is real, if modest in basis-point terms: both series now sit among the highest readings of their own past six months (Dashboard, below).

The same day's growth and inflation data landed in between the two readings that would have made the call for the broader market one way or the other: October core PCE printed in line, and the BEA's second look at Q3 GDP came in well above even the strong advance estimate (Claims, above) — strong growth, calm inflation, neither clearly behind today's credit move nor against it. Treasury yields, meanwhile, barely moved and continue to sit at levels last matched broadly in July 2007, just before that cycle's credit stress began in earnest (History, below) — not a new extreme, a persistent one, and one arriving from a much lower policy-rate and inflation starting point than 2007's.

## Concept of the day — Unit 3 — Credit spreads, segment 4 reinforcement (R), part two

**The same test, a different answer — OAS confirms this time.** Yesterday's segment ran a simple test on a concentrated chip-sector selloff: when stocks fall hard, check the extra yield lenders demand on junk and investment-grade borrowers (HY and IG OAS) before deciding whether it's a genuine risk event. Yesterday the test said no — both eased on their Monday-dated prints even as Analog Devices, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, and Micron all fell double digits. The lesson wasn't “credit never matters” — it was “check it fresh each morning.” Today, the same cap-weighted/narrow pattern repeated (the S&P 500 and Nasdaq fell again while small-caps and the equal-weight average rose), but the test flipped: HY OAS rose 9bp to 3.72% and IG OAS rose 1bp to 0.98% on their Tuesday-dated prints — small moves in basis-point terms, but enough, against a historically tight 120-day range, to push both to among the highest readings of the past six months, and HY OAS's own three-year record now sits at its 95th percentile.

**Why this matters:** nine basis points doesn't sound like much next to a multi-percent stock move, but credit spreads move in a much narrower band than stock prices, so a small move there can carry more informational weight than a large one in equities. Today's answer is the opposite of yesterday's — not because the model changed its mind, but because the data did. That's the whole discipline: re-run the test every morning, don't recycle yesterday's conclusion.

**Client translation:** "A rough day for chip stocks plus credit spreads actually widening this time — unlike yesterday — is a more serious combination, worth treating differently from yesterday's wobble even though the stock-price pattern looks almost identical."

I thought PC companies were struggling with memory costs.”** Both things are true at once: HP beat its own earnings estimate but guided cautiously because memory chips now eat roughly 40% of what it costs to build a PC, and the stock still rallied hard the next session once the initial guidance-driven dip had been digested (Movers, above) — a good reminder that a stock's first-day reaction to earnings isn't always its last word.

**“What about that inflation and growth data this week?”** Core PCE (the Fed's preferred inflation gauge) printed right in line with expectations, and Q3 GDP growth was revised up sharply — strong growth, calm inflation, and neither one clearly explains today's credit-market wobble (Claims, above). Worth saying plainly: this week's chip-sector story and this week's economic data are, so far, two separate things.
