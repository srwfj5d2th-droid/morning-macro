The story

Friday's economic data was the hottest yet. S&P Global's flash composite PMI — an early monthly survey of business activity — jumped to 56.0, the strongest reading in 52 months, with the services side at 56.8 against expectations of 54.0 (Yahoo Finance/CNBC coverage). And this time the bond market's reaction had a new feature: it wasn't just long rates that rose. The 2-year Treasury — the rate that tracks what the Fed will actually do, which had sat frozen at 4.19% through the entire week's drama — moved up 5bp to 4.24%. In plain terms: after a week of arguing only about the price of lending long, the market started to also question whether the Fed stays this easy. The 10-year rose to 4.74% and the 30-year to 5.27%, and both carry watch-list flags this morning (higher than on all but a handful of days in the past six months). One note for the record: some Friday coverage quoted the 30-year "down around 5.23%" — that was intraday; the official close (treasury.gov) was 5.27%.

The weekend then changed the subject. The tariff pause with Canada that calmed markets on Wednesday broke down — talks failed, with reciprocal tariff threats now on the table — and the administration announced new economic sanctions on Iran (TheStreet, CNBC). Futures opened the week mixed-to-lower. As always here, tariffs are treated as mechanics, not politics: they are a cost that lands somewhere — in importers' margins, in consumer prices, or in exchange rates — and "which of those three" is the analytical question for the week. Oil is the other channel: WTI sits at 87.06, up 51.6% this year, and Iran sanctions press on exactly that.

Now the cross-asset check, because it's telling an interesting story: gold rose again Friday (+2.4% to 4,624) — its fifth straight weekly gain, the longest streak since last October (Benzinga) — and inflation breakevens have quietly climbed 10bp in a week. Gold rallying while yields rise is not the usual pattern; it reads like some investors buying insurance against inflation staying sticky even as growth runs hot. Credit remains completely calm (junk spreads at 2.73%, near the year's lows), and the equal-weight S&P is again unusually strong (+1.7σ) — the average stock keeps refusing to confirm any downturn story. This week supplies the two biggest tests in months: Nvidia's earnings (the AI demand question, answered with numbers) and Jackson Hole (the Fed's own voice, after a week when markets moved without it).

Concept of the day · Unit 1 · Yield curve mechanics · segment 4 of ~10

When the front end moves, the vote is about the Fed

All week this space has leaned on one diagnostic: the 2-year Treasury didn't move, so the drama wasn't about the Fed. Friday that changed, and it's worth slowing down on why the 2-year is the tell. A 2-year note's return is dominated by the path of the Fed's overnight rate over the next 24 months — there's little room for anything else in it. So the 2-year is effectively a running poll of Fed expectations: when it jumps 5bp in a day, as it did Friday after a 52-month-high business survey, the market is saying "the odds of the Fed staying tighter for longer just went up." Long bonds carry that same expectation plus the extra compensation for time (the premium we covered in segment 2) — which is why a hot-data day moves the whole curve, while a supply announcement (Wednesday's buybacks) moves only the long end. Friday was the week's first genuine path repricing. It also reframes Jackson Hole: the market has already moved its Fed forecast; the Chair's speech now either validates that move or fights it — and either answer moves the 2-year first.

Client translation: "Watch the 2-year Treasury when big news hits: it's the market's live forecast of what the Fed does next. Last week it sat dead still through all the drama — until Friday's strong economic report nudged it up. That was the market quietly saying rate cuts just got a little less likely."
