The story

The press calls Thursday a rotation away from AI. CNBC’s live blog led with “Dow rises more than 500 points as investors rotate away from AI trade.” Reuters’ close: “S&P 500 ends higher as traders shift from AI to healthcare.” The Associated Press: “Wall Street muscles higher despite more swings for AI stocks.” On bonds, CNBC headlined “Treasury yields slide as investors flee to safety of bonds amid tech rout.” That came on a day the data ran hot. The producer price index (PPI, prices businesses get paid) rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year earlier, the biggest gains since 2022, per CNBC and Reuters reporting of the government release; this system’s calendar had +0.5% for the month. Core PPI (excluding food and energy) rose 0.7% against a 0.3% calendar consensus. Retail sales rose 0.8% against 0.3% expected; Reuters said higher gasoline prices drove the beat. Yahoo Finance summed up the puzzle: “The PPI Was Much Higher Than Expected. Odds of a Fed Rate Hike Edged Down Anyway.” This morning CNBC leads with “S&P 500 futures are little changed as index heads for losing week,” and AP headlined Asia’s session “Stocks rebound after tech sell-off and Indonesia shares sink on market downgrade warning.” The Wall Street Journal’s and Financial Times’ own pages couldn’t be reached from this system’s search, so their framing isn’t reported.

What the data shows: bonds rallied on hot data. The 10-year fell 9bp to 5.10% and the 30-year 9bp, while the 2-year fell 5bp. Per CME Group’s own FedWatch account, the odds of an October hike “dropped below 30%” by Thursday afternoon, after peaking near 53% following Wednesday’s CPI; around the PPI release they were near 34% (Schwab, CNBC). So hot inflation data came with lower yields and lower hike odds. CNBC’s explanation is a flight to safety from tech stocks; that is CNBC’s reading, and this system can’t separate it from other explanations with one day of data. The 10-year has been at or above 5.10% since Sep 23. Before this run, it was last there in July 2007 (Today in history, below).

On stocks, the data says “broad” this time, but not for AI hardware. The S&P 500 rose 0.33% to 7,744.06, its first gain in four sessions, 0.96% below its Oct 6 record. The Nasdaq Composite rose 0.08%. The average S&P stock (equal-weight RSP) rose 1.37%, against 0.28% for the cap-weighted SPY, and the Russell 2000 rose 1.38%. But the chip fund SMH fell 1.04%, its eighth straight daily decline. Software, homebuilders and health care rose; AI hardware fell again (Movers, below). Over the week, the average stock is up 0.92% while the index is down 0.87%.

The Fed goes quiet after today. Speaking with the IMF’s Kristalina Georgieva in Bangkok (late Thursday, U.S. time), Fed Chair Warsh gave no signal on October. CNBC headlined “Fed’s Warsh withholds rate signal, praises recent global tightening with ‘no free riders,’” and quoted him: “I don’t believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings that we have in the balance of this year.” CNBC also reported him saying AI’s path will be “very bumpy.” The Fed’s pre-meeting quiet period runs Oct 17–29 (Chicago Fed calendar), so today is the last day officials can comment before the Oct 27–28 meeting.

Oil is still the inflation story. WTI rose 3.03% Thursday to $97.97, up 6.66% this week. A wire report carried by Nation Thailand tied the gain to an 8.6-million-barrel weekly drop in U.S. crude inventories reported by the Energy Information Administration; that is one outlet’s stated reason, not checked against a second source. Energy stocks fell 1.58% anyway, and no outlet this system found explained the split. This morning Reuters reported oil edging lower as the U.S. weighs easing sanctions on “shadow fleet” tankers carrying non-sanctioned oil, a proposal still under discussion. Why this matters: energy ran through both of Thursday’s hot numbers. Reuters said energy made up most of the jump in producer goods prices, and that gas stations made up roughly 70% of September’s retail-sales gain, with sales excluding gasoline up only about 0.2%. For households that means pump prices, not a broad pickup in spending.

Why this matters: the 10-year is down 14bp this week, and mortgage rates tend to follow it, so home buyers got a small break in a week of hot inflation data. Savers still earn high short-term rates: the 3-month bill hasn’t budged. And for bond-fund holders, credit is the piece to watch: junk-bond spreads are wider than at any point in six months.

Concept of the day · Unit 6 — Positioning & sentiment vs. fundamentals, segment 2

Rotation or retreat? Two days, two breadth readings

Segment 1 introduced breadth: the index versus the average stock. Thursday is Wednesday’s mirror image. On Wednesday SPY (cap-weighted) fell 1.08% and RSP (equal-weight) fell 0.57%. On Thursday SPY rose 0.28% and RSP rose 1.37%. Over the two days, SPY is down 0.80% and RSP is up 0.80%. That is what a rotation looks like: money moving between groups of stocks (out of the biggest AI names, into software, homebuilders and health care), not money leaving stocks. A retreat looks different: most stocks fall together, and the worry shows up in credit. Here is the complication. Credit did react: junk-bond spreads widened 9bp on Wednesday’s selloff day (CL-0043, confirmed). So the honest reading is mostly rotation, with a credit warning attached, and the warning sits where the debt is: AI builders that borrowed heavily. Unit 6’s question applies too. A rotation changes which companies investors favor. It doesn’t, by itself, say the economy changed.

Client translation: The market didn’t really go up or down this week so much as shuffle: money left the most popular AI stocks and spread into everything else, which is why an evenly spread portfolio held up better than the headline index.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
