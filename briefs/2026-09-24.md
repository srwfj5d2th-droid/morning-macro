## Concept of the day — Unit 3, Credit spreads, segment 10 of 10 (capstone)

**The credit tie-breaker, assembled — and today happens to be the textbook case.** Two weeks of daily segments add up to one habit: when something scary happens to yields or equities, check credit before deciding how scared to be. OAS is a photograph of what lenders think default and liquidity risk look like right now — a rate spike or violent stock swing is a genuine risk event only if that photograph changes too (segments 3–4). A known, scheduled catalyst that credit shrugs off beforehand is informative in its own right (segment 5), and credit's reaction to an event that actually happened — not the headline move itself — is the real signal (segment 6); a spread that tightens, not just holds flat, is a stronger version of that signal (segment 7); one data point earns less confidence than a second independent one pointing the same way (segment 8); and even a long stretch of calm spreads is a photograph of today, never a guarantee about tomorrow (segment 9). Today hands the whole toolkit a clean live case in a single morning: every Treasury tenor flagged, three of four within a fraction of this system's most extreme readings ever recorded, and HY/IG OAS unchanged to the basis point. Applying the checklist — scheduled or shock (more shock, a surprise auction result); did credit react (no); one session or a pattern (one so far — CL-0022 tests whether it becomes one) — the plain answer: today reads as a rates story, not a credit story, which is exactly why the regime tag is "tightening" and not "stress." Unit 3 closes here; Unit 4 (liquidity plumbing) opens next session.

**Client translation:** "When yields spike, my first question isn't whether it's scary — it's whether the credit market agrees with the bond market that something's actually wrong. Today they didn't agree, and that difference is most of the reason I'm not worried about your accounts."
