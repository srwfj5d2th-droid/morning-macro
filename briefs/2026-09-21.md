## The story

Reporting this morning centers on a yield milestone and a fresh geopolitical shock. CNBC, TheStreet, and Charles Schwab report the 10-year Treasury yield closed above 5% Friday for the first time since July 2007, on a "triple witching" day when roughly $6.2 trillion of options and futures expired — both outlets note that alone makes the session more volatile. Yields were pushed higher in part by the Bank of Japan, which Schwab and Bloomberg report raised its own policy rate to a 31-year high, lifting borrowing costs globally. Over the weekend, Al Jazeera and NBC News report Yemen's Houthi rebels struck Saudi Arabia's King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh with drones — hitting jet-fuel storage tanks, the first such strike on the Saudi capital in four years — while a separate ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh was intercepted. Monday morning, Yahoo Finance and Bloomberg report U.S. futures drifting higher as oil retreats for a fourth straight session and markets look ahead to Thursday's Trump-Xi summit in Washington, trade/Taiwan/AI export policy reportedly on the agenda.

**Does the state file confirm the press's framing?** On the yield milestone, cleanly: the 10-year closed at 5.01% Friday, a genuine, script-verified crossing of the 5% line. But two threads get complicated by the data rather than confirmed. First, if a hawkish BOJ hike were tightening dollar-funding conditions, a weaker dollar would be one expected footprint — instead DXY sat flat at 100.22 both Thursday and Friday. Second, if the weekend's Houthi strike were reinforcing the same oil supply-risk premium behind this month's rally, WTI would be expected to hold or extend gains — instead WTI eased for a fourth straight session to $100.30, down from Tuesday's $105.83 peak. One plausible but untested reading: the strike hit fuel storage at an airport, not export/production infrastructure, so markets may be reading it as lower-stakes than the pipeline outage that drove the original spike — CL-0017 remains the open, falsifiable version of this question. Meanwhile last week's loudest AI-capex-scare narrative is now closed: CL-0015 refutes it outright, and this morning's SanDisk and Coinbase moves (Movers, below) extend the same "scare didn't stick" pattern.

## Concept of the day — Unit 3, Credit spreads, segment 8 of ~10

**One data point becomes two — when a repeated observation starts to earn the word "pattern."** Segment 7 found credit tightening, not just staying flat, on the Fed's hawkish surprise — logged as CL-0019 with one honest caveat: a single day is a data point, not a trend. Today answers that caveat: the next dated OAS fill arrived unchanged on both series, at the exact same level as before, to the basis point. That's the difference between a claim and a pattern — one observation can be noise; two independent observations pointing the same way start to earn confidence, precisely because the second one didn't have to agree with the first. This is also why this system logs a test condition before checking the data, not after — CL-0019 specified in advance exactly how much movement would count as "still calm" vs. "catching up late." The same logic runs the other direction on CL-0015 today: the AI-capex scare needed a second data point to become a real reassessment, and instead the second data point (SMH's continued outperformance) closed the door on it.

**Client translation:** "When something calms down once, that could just be luck. When it calms down twice in a row, at the same level, that's the market actually telling you something — which is why I don't get excited about a single quiet day, but two in a row starts to mean something."
