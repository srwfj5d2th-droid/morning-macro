Does the state file confirm the inflation-fear story? Only partially, and credit is the detail that matters most. The rate move itself is real and system-verified: the 10-year rose 3bp to 4.83% — its highest close in this system's history — and every single Tier 1 Treasury tenor this system tracks (3-month, 2-year, 10-year, 30-year) is now flagged (§4, |z| ≥ 1.5), meaning each sits higher than on all but a handful of days in the past six months. That's a genuine, broad-based move, not a headline exaggerating a small one. But the piece of the puzzle that would actually confirm "inflation fear" specifically — credit spreads, the market's own read on default and liquidity risk — didn't move that way at all. High-yield spreads (the extra yield junk-rated borrowers pay over Treasuries) eased to 2.67%, still below its own six-month average (z −0.82); investment-grade spreads held flat at 0.81%, confirming CL-0008 above. If bond investors were genuinely pricing rising inflation or credit risk, spreads are usually among the first places it shows up — and they haven't moved. The one series that would settle this cleanly — the real-yield/breakeven split (Unit 2's toolkit) — is one day behind, dated Tuesday 09-08, and doesn't yet include Wednesday's move; that's the same lag that produced last Monday's capstone lesson, replayed here as this morning's new claim (CL-0013, above), with today's Core PPI print as the first real test. Underneath the rate story, Wednesday also produced a genuine split inside the AI trade itself — see Movers, below — which argues today isn't a single, monolithic "AI capex" signal either.

Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 1 of ~10

OAS — the extra yield lenders demand, and why it's a different signal than the level of rates

Every bond has a yield, but two things determine it: the "risk-free" Treasury rate (today's whole subject above) and a spread on top, compensating a lender for the chance the borrower doesn't pay them back (default risk) or that the bond is hard to sell quickly without moving its price (liquidity risk). This system tracks that spread using OAS — option-adjusted spread — for both investment-grade (safer, larger companies) and high-yield ("junk," smaller or more leveraged companies) credit. The key idea for today: Treasury yields and credit spreads are two separate dials, not one. Treasury yields mostly reflect Fed policy expectations and how much investors want to be paid for lending money over time (today's whole story). Credit spreads reflect something else — whether lenders are worried about getting paid back at all. Wednesday is about as clean a real-world illustration of that split as this system has recorded: every Treasury tenor flagged as unusually high, while high-yield spreads actually eased and investment-grade spreads didn't move an inch. If today's rate spike reflected real fear about the economy or corporate defaults, credit would typically move first — it's historically the more sensitive, forward-looking gauge of the two. It didn't, which is a big part of why this morning's regime tag reads "tightening" (rates repricing higher) rather than "stress" (the market getting worried about being repaid).

Client translation: "Interest rates jumped again today — the most across-the-board move we've seen since I started sending you these — but the extra yield lenders demand from riskier, junk-rated borrowers barely budged. That gap matters: it's the bond market's way of saying 'borrowing got more expensive for everyone,' not 'we're worried about anyone defaulting.'"
