The story

The circulating narrative since Friday has two threads. The first: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech as chair landed more hawkish than expected — he said this summer's better inflation readings "do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved" and that markets are "showing few signs of policy restraint," language read across the wires (CNBC, Axios, PBS News) as opening the door to a rate hike rather than the cut many had expected from a new chair. Fed funds futures priced the odds of a September hike at roughly 60%, up from about 56% pre-speech. Coverage (Axios, Semafor) also noted the stance puts Warsh's likely path at odds with President Trump's public preference for lower rates — reported here as press framing, not something this brief's data can adjudicate (§4D). The second thread is newer and less settled: reporting over the weekend (Bloomberg) describes a fresh flare-up in the Strait of Hormuz, with the U.S. military striking Iranian rocket launchers Sunday that were reportedly being readied to mine the waterway, and Brent crude quoted above $90 a barrel in that coverage. This system's own oil series doesn't have a Monday print yet — Friday's close was $83.40 — so that number is reported as attributed context, not as this brief's own verified fact.

Does the state file confirm the Fed story? Cleanly, yes. All three flags on today's board (2-year, 10-year, and 30-year Treasury yields) moved the same direction Friday, with the front end moving hardest: the 2-year rose 14 basis points to 4.34% (higher than all but a handful of days in the past six months, z +1.67) while the 10-year rose only 6bp to 4.73% (z +1.69, today's largest single flag) — a curve that flattened by 8bp on the day (2s10s spread narrowed from 47bp to 39bp), the textbook signature of a hawkish repricing concentrated at the short end rather than a broad risk-off move. The dollar (DXY) firmed 0.54 points to 99.70, and gold — which tends to fall when the market prices a tighter Fed and higher real interest rates — dropped 2.85% to $4,478, the sharpest one-day move on the whole board and a clean second confirmation of the same story. Equities took it in stride rather than panicking: the S&P 500 slipped 0.25% to 7,711.76, still just 1.12% off its all-time closing high, and credit didn't confirm any stress — high-yield spreads (the extra yield lenders demand from junk-rated borrowers) actually tightened another 4bp to 2.63% (z -1.12, comfortably calm). The oil story, by contrast, the data can't confirm or refute yet — which is exactly why it's logged as CL-0007 rather than asserted as today's second headline.

Concept of the day · Unit 2 · Real vs. nominal decomposition · segment 1 of roughly 6

Splitting a Treasury yield into two prices: growth-and-inflation, and time

Unit 1 treated the 10-year Treasury yield as one number. It's actually two, added together. The real yield (measured by TIPS -- Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, bonds whose payments adjust with inflation) is roughly what investors demand to lend money for ten years after stripping out inflation -- compensation for giving up the use of their money and taking on the risk that growth disappoints. The breakeven is the market's implied average inflation rate over that same period -- literally the inflation rate at which owning a regular Treasury and an inflation-protected one would earn the same return. Add them together and you get (approximately) the plain nominal yield everyone quotes. It's not exact -- there's a small extra sliver for the liquidity difference between the two bond types -- but it's close enough to be useful. Thursday's data (08-27, the most recent date with both figures published) makes the arithmetic almost exact: TIPS real yield 2.34% + breakeven 2.33% = 4.67%, which was that same day's nominal 10-year yield to the basis point. The payoff: when the nominal yield moves, this decomposition tells you whether markets are repricing growth expectations, inflation expectations, or both -- a very different story for a bond portfolio, or a mortgage client, depending on which one moved.

Client translation: "When we say the 10-year Treasury yield, that number is really two things stacked together -- what investors want to be paid for expected inflation, and what they want to be paid on top of that for tying up money for ten years -- and separating them tells us whether a rate move is really an inflation story or a growth story."
