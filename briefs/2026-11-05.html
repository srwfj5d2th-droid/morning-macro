The story

Wednesday's dominant, named-source-attributed story is a split one. On one side, a broad market rally: value, financials, and healthcare names (Bank of America, Mastercard, Merck, Visa, Johnson & Johnson, Goldman Sachs and others all up roughly 3–4%) led the S&P 500 and Nasdaq higher. On the other, a sharp selloff in the AI-power trade: Vistra and NRG, two gas-fired power producers that have become Wall Street's proxy for AI-datacenter electricity demand, each fell more than 13% after Tuesday's Texas gubernatorial election delivered a result read as a threat to new data-center permitting, and KeyBanc analyst Sophie Karp cut Vistra to Sector Weight from Overweight on the resulting policy uncertainty (Yahoo Finance, MarketBeat, GuruFocus). The same election cycle was framed by Fortune as a broader “blue wave,” and Fortune also tied Wednesday's 8.30% crude-oil drop partly to Iran peace-talk optimism arriving the same day as the vote. Separately, and idiosyncratically, FIS (Fidelity National Information Services) fell 23.67% — the S&P 500's largest decliner — after cutting 2026 EPS guidance, flagging a preliminary 2027 EPS decline on client-implementation delays and higher technology/cybersecurity spending, and announcing its CFO's departure, despite an in-line Q3 print (Benzinga, GuruFocus). On the election result itself, this brief has nothing to say about what it means for policy — only how, mechanically, a permitting change would feed through to specific companies' cash flows, which is the question the Vistra/NRG move is actually pricing (§4D).

The state file confirms the rally was genuinely broad, not a cap-weighted illusion: the equal-weight RSP (+1.86%) actually outgained the cap-weighted SPY, the opposite of the narrow-rally pattern this brief's own curriculum has flagged before. It also confirms the AI-power-trade and chip-complex divergence directly — SMH fell 2.75% the same session equities broadly rallied, this run's single sharpest one-day split between the index and that one complex. What it complicates is any read that credit is comfortable with all of this: HY and IG OAS, the premium investors demand to lend to junk- and investment-grade borrowers, barely moved on the day but still carry this morning's two largest statistical flags, sitting in the upper reaches of their own multi-year range (Dashboard, below). Worth holding alongside all of this: the 30-year Treasury's own long-run echo of 2007 is explored in Today in History, below.

Concept of the day · Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 1

A hierarchy-of-evidence habit — what to do when bonds, credit, and equities disagree

Seven units in, this brief has built tools for reading one asset class at a time: the curve (Unit 1), real vs. nominal yields (Unit 2), credit spreads (Unit 3), liquidity plumbing (Unit 4), the dollar (Unit 5), breadth (Unit 6), and financing cycles (Unit 7). Unit 8 asks a different question: what do you do when they don't agree? The honest answer isn't “pick the one that confirms what you already thought” — it's a rough hierarchy. Credit (Unit 3's lesson) tends to be the most trustworthy tie-breaker, because lenders have money directly at risk and no reason to get swept up in a story; equity moves can be sentiment-driven and reverse fast; a single name's move (like today's FIS) is the least informative of all, since it can simply be that one company's own news. Today handed the unit a clean specimen: equities rallied broadly and the breadth measure actually confirmed it (Unit 6's tool), credit stayed flat but sits in the upper reaches of its own recent range (Dashboard, above) — neither confirming nor denying — and the AI-power trade sold off hard on a political shock that has nothing to do with either. Three readings, one morning: that's the habit this unit exists to build, not resolving the disagreement by force, but naming which asset's signal to weight more and why.

Client translation: When stocks, bonds, and credit are all saying something slightly different on the same morning, the question isn't which one is right -- it's which one has the most skin in the game, and that's usually the lender, not the buyer.

Client lens

“The market was up a lot today, so why does my statement still look rough?” If a client holds AI-power-trade names like Vistra or NRG, that's why: those fell over 13% the same day the broad market rallied, on new worries about whether data centers can get permitted fast enough in Texas. A broad-market gain doesn't mean every holding went up with it.

“Did the election cause the stock drop?” For two specific utilities, yes, mechanically: an election result raised the odds of new restrictions on permitting data centers, and an analyst downgrade followed within hours. This brief isn't weighing in on whether that policy direction is good or bad — only that it's the stated, attributed reason for those two stocks' move specifically, not a read on the whole market.

“Gas prices might finally come down?” Oil fell 8.3% Wednesday — the day's largest percentage move among the series in this morning's dashboard. If it holds, it's a genuine tailwind at the pump and for airline/transport costs — though a single day's move, even a large one, isn't yet a trend.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
