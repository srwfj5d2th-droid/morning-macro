The story

Reporting this morning centers on the bond market. The Motley Fool, Yahoo Finance, and Bloomberg report that Treasury yields marched to some of their highest levels in nearly two decades yesterday, with the 10-year note briefly touching territory last seen in 2007 and the 5-year note crossing 5% for the first time since that year. Bloomberg reports the proximate trigger was a weak $70 billion sale of new 5-year notes — when the Treasury auctions new debt and investors demand a higher yield than expected to buy it, that "soft" auction result itself pushes yields higher across the board, separate from any economic news. Yahoo Finance and Invezz add that the move accelerated after September's preliminary (flash) manufacturing and services activity readings came in stronger than forecast, which traders read as reducing the odds the Fed can afford to hold rates steady much longer; Invezz reports the Dow fell roughly 350 points on the day. Reporting attributes part of the decline in rate-sensitive stocks specifically to this dynamic: Yahoo Finance's market recap notes utilities and real-estate stocks (REITs) — both sectors valued partly for their dividends, which compete directly with what a Treasury bond now pays — led sector losses.

Does the state file confirm the press's framing? Cleanly, and about as unambiguously as this system's four-week history has produced. Every single Tier 1 rate series is flagged this morning — the 3-month bill, 2-year, 10-year, and 30-year Treasury yields all carry a reading higher than on all but a handful of days in the past six months, with the 2-year and 10-year each within a fraction of this system's most extreme readings ever recorded (Dashboard, below). The move's shape matches the reporting exactly: yesterday's single-day jump in the 2-year (+14bp) was larger on its own than the entire prior five trading days combined (+11bp net) — a curve that had essentially gone nowhere for a week erased all of that calm in one session, consistent with an auction-and-data shock landing on a single day rather than a slow-building trend. The one piece of the tape that did not confirm a "something's breaking" read: credit. High-yield and investment-grade spreads (the extra yield lenders demand from riskier borrowers) were both unchanged on their freshest dated print, exactly the OAS tie-breaker this system's curriculum has spent two weeks building (Concept, below) — a sharp rate move without a credit reaction reads as a rates story, not a risk-appetite story. Equities confirmed the same read at a smaller scale: the S&P 500 fell 0.75% and the cap-weighted and equal-weighted versions of the index fell almost identically (SPY -0.72%, RSP -0.64%), a broad, orderly pullback rather than a narrow, panicked one. One open, falsifiable question the state file can't yet answer: whether this was a one-day auction-specific distortion or the start of a durable higher-rate regime — CL-0022 (Claims, above) is the test.

Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 10 of 10 (capstone)

The credit tie-breaker, assembled — and today happens to be the textbook case

Two weeks of daily segments add up to one habit: when something scary happens to yields or equities, check credit before deciding how scared to be. OAS (the extra yield lenders charge riskier borrowers over a Treasury) is a photograph of what lenders think default and liquidity risk look like right now — a rate spike or a violent stock swing is a genuine risk event only if that photograph changes too (segments 3-4). A known, scheduled catalyst that credit shrugs off beforehand is informative in its own right (segment 5), and credit's reaction to an event that actually happened — not the headline move itself — is the real signal (segment 6); a spread that tightens, not just holds flat, is a stronger version of that same signal (segment 7); one data point earns less confidence than a second independent one pointing the same way (segment 8); and even a long stretch of calm spreads is a photograph of today, never a guarantee about tomorrow (segment 9, the honest limit on all of this). Today happens to hand the whole toolkit a clean live case in a single morning: every Treasury tenor flagged, three of four sitting within a fraction of this system's most extreme readings ever recorded, and HY/IG OAS unchanged to the basis point (Dashboard, above). Applying the full checklist: is it scheduled or a shock (a surprise auction result, so more shock than schedule)? Did credit react at all (no)? Is this one session or a pattern (one session so far — CL-0022 now exists to test whether it becomes a pattern)? The answer the checklist gives, plainly: today reads as a rates story, not a credit story — which is exactly why the regime tag is "tightening" and not "stress." That's Unit 3, closed. Unit 4 (liquidity plumbing — bank reserves, the Fed's balance sheet, and how a Treasury refunding decision moves markets before the Fed does anything at all) opens next.

Client translation: "When yields spike, my first question isn't whether it's scary — it's whether the credit market agrees with the bond market that something's actually wrong. Today they didn't agree, and that difference is most of the reason I'm not worried about your accounts."
