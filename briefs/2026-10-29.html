The story

The Fed held, but not everyone agreed, and that split matters more than the hold itself. The FOMC kept its target range at 3.75%–4.00% on Wednesday, a 10-2 vote; Fed Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike (Federal Reserve, CNBC, Fox Business). The statement kept its “inflation remains elevated” language while upgrading its read on growth to a “strong” pace, and both TD Economics and Seeking Alpha read the pause as raising, not lowering, the odds of a December hike. That's the test for CL-0052 (Claims, above), opened Tuesday on whether this run's credit and funding-cost flags are policy-driven or technical: the dissent and the growth upgrade point toward policy-driven, though the Treasury/SOFR legs of that specific test are still mixed with one session left. The 10-year, at 5.05%, now sits at a level the market last held on a sustained basis before 2007 (History, below) — a reminder that “tightening” here means the cost of borrowing, not a crisis.

Underneath the Fed story, two AI-capex earnings reports made the morning's clearer macro statement — and they didn't agree with each other on how. Vertiv jumped 16.48% after Q3 adjusted EPS of $2.21 beat the roughly $1.82 consensus, net sales grew 61% to $4.31B, and organic orders more than doubled year-over-year; Executive Chairman Dave Cote tied the beat directly to accelerating data-center demand (Yahoo Finance, Motley Fool, TradingView/StockStory). Oracle rose 10.73%, not on an earnings print but on new long-term targets set at an investor day: a return to positive free cash flow by the second half of fiscal 2028, $30B-plus free cash flow by fiscal 2029, $225B revenue by fiscal 2030, and a cap on new capital raises through fiscal 2030 (Yahoo Finance, StocksToTrade, TradingKey) — the market rewarding a stated self-funding timeline, not just more spending, which is exactly this week's financing-cycle lens (Concept of the day, below). Not every hardware name agreed: F5 fell 20.43% despite beating its own Q4 estimates, on guidance implying a slower FY2027 and a decelerating hardware-refresh cycle (GuruFocus, Fool.com, TradingView/StockStory), and Fortinet fell 5.90% the same day with no distinct catalyst this system could find — logged today as new conditional claim CL-0054 (Flags, below) rather than assumed to be the same story as Vertiv's and Oracle's. The broader chip/AI-hardware basket (Lam Research, Applied Materials, Seagate, Western Digital, Micron, AMD, Broadcom — Movers/Tier 3, below) gained again, continuing the recovery CL-0049 (confirmed today, Claims above) called a rotation rather than a retreat two weeks ago.

Put together: a hawkish dissent, a 2007-era 10-year, and two AI-capex names making opposite-flavored but both-constructive cases all point the same direction as this run's own credit/dollar/funding flags — tightening, confirmed by the data rather than asserted by the headline. Today's own data (GDP, Core PCE, jobless claims, all 8:30am ET) is the next real test of whether the Fed's growth upgrade holds up (Calendar, below).

For a client asking if 5% on the 10-year means something's breaking: no — today's Fed is less restrictive than the last time rates were here, the more reassuring read, not the alarming one.

Concept of the day · Unit 7 — Financing cycles & reflexivity, segment 4

Demand-side evidence vs. financing-side evidence — reading Vertiv's orders against Oracle's cash-flow timeline

Segments 1-3 built two tools: the Minsky sequence for judging whether a capex boom can fund itself, and the distinction between a broad credit flag and an AI-specific credit story (yesterday's segment). Today supplies a clean example of a third distinction: demand-side evidence versus financing-side evidence. Vertiv's organic orders more than doubling year-over-year (Movers, above) is demand-side — it says buyers are actually placing orders for data-center power and cooling equipment, not just talking about AI capex. Oracle's new free-cash-flow timeline is financing-side — it says nothing new about demand, but it's a company telling investors how its own build-out gets paid for, and capping new capital raises is a specific, checkable commitment, not a vague reassurance. A capex cycle needs both to hold up over time: demand that's real (Vertiv's kind of evidence) and financing that doesn't require the credit window to stay open forever (Oracle's kind). Seeing one without the other is actually the more common pattern in capex cycles that later go bad — demand without a funding plan, or a funding plan without real orders behind it. Today happened to supply one of each, from two different companies, on the same day.

Client translation: When a company's earnings move the stock, ask whether the news was about more orders coming in or about how they plan to pay for what they're already building -- they're both good news, but they answer different client questions.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
