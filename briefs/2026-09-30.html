The story

The press framing this morning is still the Treasury move: CNBC and Yahoo Finance report the 10-year touched a session high above 5.29% yesterday and the 30-year climbed as high as 5.6%, a level reporting ties to 2002 for the 30-year and 2007 for the 10-year. Does this system's own data confirm that picture, or complicate it? Both, in different places. The rates side confirms cleanly: every Tier 1 Treasury tenor this system tracks is flagged again today (Dashboard, below), consistent with the multi-decade-high framing. But the equity reaction was far milder than a recap built around “record yields plus a 12-year-low confidence print” might suggest — this system's own S&P 500 close was essentially flat, down just 0.17% (Recap, above), still up 12.1% year-to-date and only 1.64% off its closing high. What's genuinely new since yesterday's brief is credit: investment-grade spreads joined high-yield in flag territory for the first time (Dashboard, Claims, above), one basis point short of this system's own test for whether that's a real broadening. Concept of the day · Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, segment 4

QT versus the TGA drain — two ways to shrink reserves that look identical in the reserves identity but mean different things

Segment 3's identity said reserves ≈ the Fed's balance sheet minus the TGA minus the ON RRP — and showed a big TGA build (from a Treasury refunding) draining reserves without the Fed doing anything. But there's a second way reserves shrink: the Fed's own balance-sheet runoff, often called quantitative tightening or QT, where the Fed lets bonds mature without buying new ones to replace them. Both show up as the same falling number in the identity; they are not the same event. A TGA-driven drain is temporary and mechanical — the cash sits in the government's account until it's spent back out, and reserves refill. A QT-driven drain is the Fed's own deliberate, ongoing policy choice, and it doesn't refill on its own. This week's own numbers (Dashboard, above, all thin history) show which one is actually happening right now: the Fed's balance sheet itself barely moved, +$1.156 billion week over week, while the TGA alone moved nearly a hundred times that, +$100.056 billion. This week's reserve pressure is almost entirely the Treasury's doing, not the Fed actively shrinking its balance sheet — a distinction that matters because one of these reverses itself in weeks and the other doesn't reverse until the Fed decides to stop it.

Client translation: When the news says the Fed is "still doing QT," that's a separate, slower story from a big Treasury bond sale temporarily soaking up cash from the banking system — and this week, the numbers say it's almost entirely the second one, which tends to ease up on its own once the government spends that cash back out.

Client lens

“My credit score company's stock just dropped 26% — is something wrong with FICO scores, or my mortgage?” Likely to come up given the size of the move and the brand recognition (Movers, above). No — this is a change to how Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac price a fee they charge lenders, opening the door to a rival credit-score provider (VantageScore) alongside FICO, not a change to how credit scores themselves work or a statement about mortgage credit quality.

“I keep hearing consumers are struggling, but Carnival just posted record earnings — which one is true?” A natural question given both stories broke within a day of each other (Story, Movers, above). Both, in different slices of the same economy: survey-based confidence measures how people say they feel, which fell to a 12-year low on fuel prices and hiring anxiety; actual bookings and spending, at least in cruise and travel, came in at record levels. The gap between how people say they feel and what they actually do with their money is exactly the kind of thing this system flags rather than resolves for you.

“If today's inflation report comes in hot, does that mean rates keep climbing and my bond funds keep losing value?” Timely given Core PCE lands this morning (Calendar, above). Directionally yes if it's hot and the market reads it that way — a hot print would tend to push yields higher, which is a further mark-to-market hit for existing bond funds, unchanged mechanics from every prior week this system has covered. What's different to watch for this time: whether it's the first print to actually move the bond market's own inflation forecast (the breakeven, which has sat flat all month), versus just adding to the same real-rate story that's been driving the move so far — a distinction this system will test explicitly (CL-0026, Claims, above) rather than call from the headline alone.
