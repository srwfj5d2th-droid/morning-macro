The story

The circulating narrative this morning is squarely Nvidia. Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $96.2 billion (up 18% from the prior quarter, up 106% from a year ago), with data-center revenue of $89 billion — 92% of total sales — beating the roughly $86.3 billion Wall Street had modeled (CNBC). Concept of the day · Unit 1 · Yield curve mechanics · segment 7 of ~10

The term premium — what's left over once you subtract the expected path

Every Treasury yield can be split into two pieces: what investors expect the Fed to do with short-term rates over that bond's life (the "path"), and everything else investors demand for the uncertainty of being locked in that long — inflation surprises, supply, changing appetite for duration. That second piece is the term premium, and it's the tell for telling path-moves from premium-moves. A path move shows up mostly at the front end, where Fed expectations live, and can leave the long end alone (Unit 1, segment 2's Treasury-buyback example). A term-premium move tends to show up everywhere at once, roughly in parallel, because it isn't a view about any one Fed meeting — it's a view about the whole future. Yesterday's data point was small but textbook: the 2-year and 10-year both rose the same 2 basis points. That's not the Fed-expectations market repricing a specific decision; it's closer to a uniform, term-premium-flavored nudge, the kind that's easy to miss precisely because it doesn't announce itself the way a single Fed-meeting repricing does.

Client translation: "When your whole bond ladder's yields tick up by about the same small amount on the same day, that's usually the market asking for a little more compensation for tying up money for a long time — not a sign the Fed is about to move — so I wouldn't read next month's Fed decision into a move like yesterday's."
