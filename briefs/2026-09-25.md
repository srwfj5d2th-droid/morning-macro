## Concept of the day — Unit 4, Liquidity plumbing, segment 1

**Where bank reserves actually come from — and why a $100B Treasury cash build is a plumbing fact, not a mystery.** Unit 3 closed yesterday on "does credit confirm the rate story?" Unit 4 opens on where cash in the banking system actually comes from and goes. Three accounts do the work: the Fed's balance sheet ($6.75T) is the pool of bank reserves, shrinking slowly as the Fed lets bonds mature without replacing them (QT). The Treasury General Account (TGA) is the government's own checking account — when the government sells new debt (this week's $70B five-year auction) or collects taxes, cash moves out of the banking system into TGA; when spent, it moves back. The overnight reverse repo facility (ON RRP) used to cushion this — money funds parked excess cash there instead of the banking system — but it's now nearly drained (Weekly liquidity panel, above), so a $100B TGA build in one week increasingly shows up as a direct, usually temporary, reduction in bank reserves. Not a forecast of stress — just how the plumbing works, and the mechanism this unit will use to trace a Treasury refunding decision through to what a client's money-market fund actually feels.

**Client translation:** "When the Treasury sells a big batch of new bonds, like it did this week, that cash sits in the government's own account for a bit before it's spent back out — it's a timing mechanic, not a sign of a cash crunch, and one of the quieter reasons short-term borrowing costs have stayed firm even though nothing about the economy itself changed this week."
