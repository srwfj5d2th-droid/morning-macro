The narrative is a week old; Tuesday was the equity market catching up to it.

And the equity damage was surgical, not systemic. The data reads as a crowded-trade unwind priced off higher discount rates — hypothesis CL-0002, graded within the week — not broad risk-off. The minutes at 2pm are the next test (CL-0001).

## Concept of the day — Unit 1 · Yield curve mechanics · segment 1

**The curve is a forecast, not a price list.** Start with what a Treasury yield actually is: the market-clearing price of time. This morning the overnight rate (SOFR) is 3.65% — that one the Fed effectively administers. But the 2Y at 4.19%, the 10Y at 4.71%, the 30Y at 5.28% — nobody sets those. Each is an auction-priced forecast: the average overnight rate the market expects over that horizon, *plus* a premium for bearing the uncertainty of holding paper that long. So when this week's headlines say "rates hit a two-decade high," read it precisely: the Fed has done nothing; the market has raised the price of *long-dated* time. The gap between the 2Y and 10Y — 2s10s, +52bp this morning — is the curve's slope, and its direction is a sentence: a steepening curve with the front end pinned (exactly this week's shape) says "policy is where we thought, but holding long bonds now requires more compensation." Every curve move decomposes into those two parts — expected path, and premium — and telling them apart is the next segment's job.

**Client translation:** "When you hear 'rates hit a 20-year high,' notice the Fed hasn't raised anything — the market repriced long-term borrowing. That's why your money-market yield didn't move this week, but long bond funds did."
