The story

Reporting this morning centers on a yield milestone and a fresh geopolitical shock. CNBC, TheStreet, and Charles Schwab report the 10-year Treasury yield closed above 5% on Friday for the first time since July 2007, on a "triple witching" day when roughly $6.2 trillion of options and futures expired — a session both outlets describe as inherently more volatile on that basis alone. Yields were pushed higher in part by the Bank of Japan, which Schwab and Bloomberg report raised its own policy rate to a 31-year high, lifting borrowing costs globally. Over the weekend, Al Jazeera and NBC News report Yemen's Houthi rebels struck Saudi Arabia's King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh with drones — hitting jet-fuel storage tanks, the first such strike on the Saudi capital in four years — while a separate ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh was intercepted by Saudi-led coalition defenses. Monday morning, Yahoo Finance and Bloomberg report U.S. stock futures drifting higher as oil retreats for a fourth straight session and markets look ahead to Thursday's summit between President Trump and China's Xi Jinping in Washington, with trade, Taiwan, and AI export policy reportedly on the agenda.

Does the state file confirm the press's framing? On the yield milestone, cleanly: the 10-year closed at 5.01% Friday (Dashboard, below), a genuine, script-verified crossing of the 5% line and this system's own confirmation of the "highest since 2007" framing. But two threads in this morning's reporting get complicated by the data rather than confirmed by it. First, if a hawkish BOJ hike were tightening dollar-funding conditions the way the "yields higher everywhere" framing implies, a weaker dollar would be one expected footprint — instead DXY (this system's dollar index) sat completely flat at 100.22 both Thursday and Friday. Not a refutation of the BOJ story, just a footprint this system's own data doesn't show yet. Second, if the weekend's Houthi strike on Saudi infrastructure were reinforcing the same supply-risk premium behind this month's oil rally, WTI would be expected to hold or extend its gains — instead this system's own series shows WTI easing for a fourth straight session into Friday's close, $100.30, down from Tuesday's $105.83 peak (Claims, above). One plausible reading, not asserted as fact: the strike hit an airport's fuel storage, not export or production infrastructure, so markets may be reading it as a lower-stakes escalation than the pipeline outage that drove the original spike — but that reading isn't tested here, and CL-0017 remains the open, falsifiable version of this question. Meanwhile last week's loudest AI-capex-scare narrative is now closed: CL-0015 (Claims, above) refutes it outright, and this morning's SanDisk and Coinbase moves (Movers, below) extend that same "scare didn't stick" pattern to two more names.

Every Treasury tenor stays flagged for an eighth straight session, and the 10-year crossed a line it hasn't crossed in almost two decades. The 10-year note (the benchmark that anchors mortgage rates and most long-term borrowing) rose 7bp Friday to 5.01% — its first close above 5% since July 2007, and a reading higher than on all but a handful of days in this system's history (z +2.63). The 2-year (the market's live poll of where the Fed is headed) rose further too, 9bp to 4.76% — erasing all of Thursday's give-back and then some, and directly relevant to CL-0018's test of whether the dot plot's repricing is holding (Claims, above). The 3-month bill eased its climb only slightly (+2bp to 4.14%, z +3.03, still this system's most extreme reading) while the 30-year rose 5bp to 5.34% (z +1.91). In real terms: anyone shopping for a mortgage, renewing a CD, or holding a long-duration bond fund is now pricing off borrowing costs last seen before the 2008 financial crisis. The curve's shape kept compressing: 2s10s (the 2-year-to-10-year gap, a read on how much economic slack the market expects) fell another 2bp to 25bp and stays flagged (z -2.42) — the front end is catching up to the long end faster than the long end is climbing, a flattening pattern consistent with a market still digesting a hawkish, not dovish, Fed. Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 8 of ~10

One data point becomes two — when a repeated observation starts to earn the word "pattern"

Segment 7 (Friday) found something stronger than flat credit: spreads that actually tightened on the Fed's hawkish surprise, logged as CL-0019 with one honest caveat — a single day of tightening is a data point, not a trend. Today answers that caveat directly. The next dated OAS fill arrived unchanged on both series, not just calm again but calm at the exact same level as before — 2.70% and 0.78%, to the basis point. That is the difference between a claim and a pattern: one observation can always be noise, a fluke, a stale print; two independent observations pointing the same way start to earn more confidence, precisely because the second one didn't have to agree with the first. This is also why this system logs a test condition before checking the data, not after (§4C) — CL-0019 specified in advance exactly how much movement would count as "still calm" versus "catching up late," so today's confirmation isn't a story being fit to a convenient number after the fact. The same logic runs the other direction on CL-0015 today: last week's AI-capex scare needed a second data point to become a real reassessment, and instead the second data point (SMH's continued outperformance) closed the door on it. One point is a lead. Two is a pattern — or a resolved false alarm.

Client translation: "When something calms down once, that could just be luck. When it calms down twice in a row, at the same level, that's the market actually telling you something — which is why I don't get excited about a single quiet day, but two in a row starts to mean something."
