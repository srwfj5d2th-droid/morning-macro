The story

Stocks bounced Monday, led by the names that fell hardest last week. The Nasdaq rose roughly 1.1% to 1.4% and the S&P 500 roughly 0.4% to 0.7%, depending on the outlet (TheStreet, 24/7 Wall St.); this system's own close-to-close data puts the S&P 500 at +0.41% and the Nasdaq-100 at +1.11%, in the same range. The press's stated reason doesn't fully hold up against this system's own numbers. 24/7 Wall St. tied the chip rebound to easing Treasury yields and oil prices. This system's own closes show the opposite on a close-to-close basis: the 10-year Treasury rose 6bp to 5.15% and WTI crude rose $2.25 (+2.2%) to $103.66, both up, not down. The easing the press describes may have been true intraday, or earlier in the session, and faded by the close; either way, this system records the close-to-close fact and flags the mismatch rather than folding the press's framing into its own voice untested.

Underneath the one-day bounce, the tightening backdrop didn't move. Junk-bond spreads (HY OAS, still dated Friday) are at 3.31%, this run's own largest flag for a fourth straight session (z +3.75); investment-grade spreads are also still flagged. Last week's AI-financing-stress story — CoreWeave's and Oracle's cost of insuring their own debt at fresh records (Reuters, S&P Global, dated 10-15) — hasn't been undone by one green session in chip stocks. CL-0042 and CL-0046 are this system's own tests of whether it holds.

Concept of the day · Unit 6 — Positioning & sentiment vs. fundamentals, segment 4

A rally can be just as narrow as a selloff

Segments 1 through 3 used breadth to read selloffs: a cap-weighted index falling harder than the equal-weight average says the selling was concentrated in a few giant stocks, not broad-based. The same tool works in reverse on an up day. Monday looked like “stocks rallied” by any index headline — but the equal-weight S&P fund (RSP) actually fell 0.32% on the day while the cap-weighted S&P fund (SPY) rose 0.42%, a gap of about 0.74 percentage points. That split is only possible if the gains were concentrated in a small number of very large stocks pulling the index average up while the typical company in it didn't participate — and Monday's movers list (above) is almost entirely large, AI-adjacent names: Western Digital, SanDisk, Amazon, Marvell. A narrow rally isn't fake, but it's a different fact than “the market recovered”: it says a specific trade recovered, not sentiment broadly. The same breadth check that flagged concentrated selling two weeks ago (segments 1 and 2) flags concentrated buying today — one tool, either direction.

Client translation: When the news says stocks bounced back, the honest follow-up is whether that means most of your holdings bounced, or just the handful of giant AI-related names that dominate the index headlines — today, it was mostly the latter.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
