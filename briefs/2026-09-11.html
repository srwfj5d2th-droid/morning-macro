Reuters and CNBC both led with the oil move — Brent crude (the international benchmark; this system tracks the U.S. benchmark, WTI, instead, so the two won't match dollar-for-dollar) pushed above $107 on intensifying reported security risk around the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait, two chokepoints a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through — pulling Treasury yields higher alongside it and lifting the dollar. Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 2 of ~10

Default premium vs. liquidity premium — the two things OAS is actually paying you for, and why one company's credit story doesn't move the aggregate

Yesterday's concept introduced OAS — the extra yield a lender demands over the Treasury rate — as one number. It's really compensation for two different worries bundled together. The default premium pays for the chance the borrower doesn't pay you back at all. The liquidity premium pays for the fact that a given bond may be hard to sell quickly without moving its price, even if the borrower is perfectly good for the money — a cost that has nothing to do with creditworthiness and everything to do with how thin trading in that specific bond is. The series this system tracks, HY OAS and IG OAS, are each an average across hundreds of bonds — a broad temperature reading, not a single borrower's chart. That's the key idea for today, and Oracle (see Movers and Tier 3, above) is a clean live example: Oracle's own credit story clearly deteriorated this week — a rating downgrade to one notch above junk, tens of billions in new debt planned, free cash flow staying negative for years — which is exactly the kind of thing that should widen an individual bond's default premium. But the aggregate investment-grade OAS this system tracks didn't move at all. One borrower's story, however large, doesn't move a diversified average unless the market starts pricing it as the leading edge of something broader. Watching whether that changes — whether Oracle-style stories start showing up in the aggregate, not just the headlines — is exactly what this system's OAS series is built to catch.

Client translation: "Oracle's own borrowing costs are a real story this week — a credit downgrade, tens of billions in new debt to fund AI data centers — but the broad measure of what companies overall pay to borrow hasn't budged. That's the difference between one company's story and a market-wide one, and right now, this is still just one company's."
