## Today in history

**The 10-year Treasury yield is at its highest since June 2007.** At 5.22%, that's 19 years. Against all history since 1962 it is still middle-of-the-pack — the 49th percentile — because the 1970s and '80s ran far higher. Against the last 30 years it is the 87th percentile. Both are true. The second is the world anyone with a mortgage, a bond fund, or a client book since the mid-1990s has actually lived in. The 30-year bond, at 5.60%, is at its highest since December 2001. (Treasury stopped issuing 30-year bonds from 2002 to 2006, so that window can't be checked.)

**What came after, the last time.** In June 2007 the Fed's own rate was 5.28%, so a 5.2% 10-year was mostly just tracking the Fed. Today SOFR (the Fed-driven overnight rate) is 3.88% — more than a point lower — yet the 10-year is just as high. That gap is investors asking for extra pay to lock money up for a decade: the term premium from Unit 1. NBER's recession indicator switched on in January 2008, and the S&P 500 was 10.7% lower a year after June 2007. That is one episode, not a pattern, and not a forecast. The Fed, the economy, and the reason yields are high are all different now.

**The real yield has been here only once before — during a crisis.** The 10-year real yield (what a Treasury pays after inflation) is 2.92%, the 99.7th percentile of its history since 2003. The only other stretch this high was October–November 2008, peaking at 3.15%, in the middle of the financial crisis. Back then the Fed was cutting hard: its rate fell 3.94 points over that year. Today the Fed is raising rates. Same number, opposite situation. The only historical match is not a comparable economy, so it tells you how rare this is, not what comes next.

**One combination today has no precedent in this data.** Real yields this high, together with investment-grade credit spreads this tight, has never happened in the 23 years of TIPS history. The credit gauge here is the long-run proxy — Moody's Baa corporate yield minus the 10-year — used for direction and timing only, since the live IG OAS series has just three years of history in this system. That proxy is 1.46 points, the 3rd percentile of 40 years. It was this tight or tighter only in 1988–90, 1994–95, 1996–97, and 2024–25. In plain terms: money has rarely cost this much after inflation, and lenders have rarely charged corporate borrowers so little extra for the risk. The one prior time real yields were this high, in late 2008, that same credit gauge was nowhere near tight: its peak during the 2007–09 crisis window was 6.16 points. This is the same split claim CL-0036 is grading day by day (Claims, above). History says it is genuinely unusual — a reason to keep watching it, not a call on how it resolves.

**Stocks near a record with the 10-year this high: last seen in 2000.** The S&P 500 is within 0.7% of its record close. Pair that with a 5.22% 10-year and the last time both were true was September 2000, the end of a stretch that began in February 1995 — the dot-com years. Before then it was common (7.1% of all trading days since 1962). Since then it has not happened at all. In the year after September 2000 the S&P 500 fell 25.5%. Again, one episode. But it is the historical company today's AI-capex story keeps (Tier 3, below, and Unit 7's financing-cycle lens). A 10-year this high with a dollar this strong also last held together in 1998–2002.

**Balance: not every match ended badly.** After the real yield's 2008 spike, the S&P 500 rose 29.8% over the next year. And the yield curve's 2022–24 inversion — at 783 days, the longest of the 18 material 2s10s inversions since 1976 — has passed its three-year window with no recession. Of those 18, 14 were followed by a recession within three years and 4 were not. The curve (2s10s, +47bp) has now been this flat or flatter on and off since February 2022.

**Today's market moves, in history.** The Nasdaq's 1.25% drop was its biggest since August 18. But drops that size come about 37 days a year over the last 30 years — roughly three a month. The S&P 500's 0.47% dip happens about 64 days a year. Year to date the S&P is up 13.4%, 37th of 99 years through this date; the typical year was up 7.5%. A good year, not a historic one. Oil is the outlier: WTI's 59.3% gain this year is the 4th-largest by this date since 2001. Gold sits 21.8% below the record close it set in January.

**The dollar, for scale.** Yesterday's brief called DXY's 102.24 a new high “in this system's own data.” With real history behind it, today's 102.14 is the 71st percentile since 1971 and the 81st over the last 30 years. It was this high as recently as April 2025, and it reached 114.11 in September 2022. Elevated, not extreme.

**Why this matters:** “Are rates high?” now has an honest two-part answer for clients: not by 1980s standards, but yes by the standards of every mortgage and bond fund of the last 30 years — the highest 10-year since 2007. And the market's current mix — high real rates, calm credit, stocks near records — is rare. The last times the 10-year was this high (2007), or this high with stocks at a record (2000), a recession began within two years. Two instances: worth knowing, not a law and not a forecast, and the 2022–24 curve inversion is the recent reminder that history's patterns can skip a turn.

## Concept of the day — Unit 5 — The dollar cycle, segment 3

**Global transmission: a strong dollar is exported tightening.** The first two segments covered why a stronger dollar tightens conditions mechanically, and why carry (U.S. rates paying more than other countries') is a big reason the dollar strengthens in the first place. Today's piece is about who feels that tightening even though the Fed never touched a rate. Two channels do the work. First, any government, bank, or company outside the U.S. that borrowed in dollars — a very common choice for emerging-market borrowers — owes the same dollar amount back, but now costs more of their own local currency to repay as the dollar strengthens; a strong dollar is, functionally, a tax on the rest of the world's dollar debts. Second, globally traded commodities (including oil, §5 — up 3.6% today on its own separate story) are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes the same barrel more expensive for a buyer paying in euros, yen, or rupees, squeezing growth abroad even before any local inflation shows up. For scale, with real history now behind DXY: today's 102.14 is the 81st percentile of the last 30 years, but the dollar was at 114.11 as recently as September 2022, so this week's strength is meaningful without being extreme. Today is also a small counter-example worth noticing: the dollar eased slightly (DXY −0.10%, Dashboard above) alongside Treasury yields, so this specific channel loosened a touch even on a day when a different story (the AI-stock selloff, Story above) dominated the headlines — a reminder that the dollar-tightening channel and the day's dominant narrative don't always move together.

**Client translation:** "When the dollar is strong, it acts like a hidden tax on foreign borrowers and on anyone buying oil or other commodities priced in dollars — it tightens the squeeze on the rest of the world even when the Fed hasn't lifted a finger."
