## Concept of the day — Unit 5 — The dollar cycle, segment 3

**Global transmission: a strong dollar is exported tightening.** The first two segments covered why a stronger dollar tightens conditions mechanically, and why carry (U.S. rates paying more than other countries') is a big reason the dollar strengthens in the first place. Today's piece is about who feels that tightening even though the Fed never touched a rate. Two channels do the work. First, any government, bank, or company outside the U.S. that borrowed in dollars — a very common choice for emerging-market borrowers — owes the same dollar amount back, but now costs more of their own local currency to repay as the dollar strengthens; a strong dollar is, functionally, a tax on the rest of the world's dollar debts. Second, globally traded commodities (including oil, §5 — up 3.6% today on its own separate story) are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes the same barrel more expensive for a buyer paying in euros, yen, or rupees, squeezing growth abroad even before any local inflation shows up. Today is actually a small counter-example worth noticing: the dollar eased slightly (DXY −0.10%, Dashboard above) alongside Treasury yields, so this specific channel loosened a touch even on a day when a different story (the AI-stock selloff, Story above) dominated the headlines — a reminder that the dollar-tightening channel and the day's dominant narrative don't always move together.

**Client translation:** "When the dollar is strong, it acts like a hidden tax on foreign borrowers and on anyone buying oil or other commodities priced in dollars — it tightens the squeeze on the rest of the world even when the Fed hasn't lifted a finger."
