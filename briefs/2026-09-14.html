The story

Friday's press coverage centered on one number: Thursday's August Core CPI (the inflation gauge that excludes food and energy, watched closely because it strips out the most volatile prices) rose 0.3% for the month, above the roughly 0.2% consensus. CNBC and Bloomberg both reported the reaction was immediate and large — the 10-year Treasury yield touched an intraday high of about 4.95%, and Bloomberg reported bond traders moved to pricing two Fed rate hikes by year-end, with FXStreet putting Wednesday's hike probability at roughly 90%. That's a genuine reversal in the story this system has been tracking all month: the debate just three weeks ago (CL-0006) was whether the Fed would hold or cut; by Friday the debate was whether it hikes once or twice. Stocks didn't sell off on that news, though — The Motley Fool, Yahoo Finance, and Seeking Alpha all led Friday's market wrap with a different story: blowout earnings from Dell, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, and NetApp (all three detailed in Movers, below), each reporting record AI-server or AI-storage demand and raising guidance, which multiple outlets credited with keeping the S&P 500 and chip stocks green even as yields climbed. Then Monday morning brought a new, weekend-sourced thread: CNBC and Bloomberg both reported Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for an industry-wide slowdown in frontier AI development over safety concerns, while OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said the company would not pursue an IPO this year, calling it "ill-advised" — reporting credits the combination, alongside rising oil prices, with pulling S&P 500 futures down roughly 0.8% and Nasdaq-100 futures down roughly 1.8% ahead of the open. Worth flagging rather than adopting (§4C): that futures move is a pre-market, not-yet-a-close data point; this system's own state file won't have a verifiable read on it until tomorrow's pull, which is exactly why CL-0015 (above) logs it as a test rather than a conclusion.

Does the state file confirm "the Fed is turning hawkish," and does Friday's rally complicate it? The rates side confirms cleanly. All four Treasury tenors this system tracks are flagged (§4, |z| ≥ 1.5) for a third straight session, and the shape of the move is the tell: the 3-month bill and 2-year note — the maturities most sensitive to what the Fed actually does next, a distinction this curriculum's Unit 1 called "the path" — posted this system's two largest flags ever recorded (3-month z +3.08, 2-year z +2.92) and both closed at fresh series highs. The 30-year, the maturity that prices term premium and long-run growth/inflation more than near-term Fed moves, actually eased 2bp on the day and isn't at a fresh high. That's a front-end-led move, textbook language for "the market is repricing what the Fed itself will do," not a broad-based selloff. The curve's shape says the same thing from a second angle: 2s10s (the 2-year-to-10-year gap) narrowed 6bp to 33bp, flagged (z −1.57) — a flattening move consistent with the front end catching up to a hawkish repricing. Credit still hasn't moved: high-yield spreads eased further (z −0.60) and investment-grade spreads stayed flat (z +0.38), both unflagged — the market that prices default and liquidity risk isn't (yet) treating a Fed hike as a threat to growth, which is exactly why today's tag stays "tightening" rather than "stress." Friday's equity rally is real in this system's own numbers too: the S&P 500 rose 0.86%, the chip-fund proxy SMH rose 1.47%, both moving the opposite direction from the Treasury selloff — the state file backs up the press's "earnings offset the rate move" read for Friday specifically. What it can't yet do is confirm or complicate Monday's AI-caution headlines, since those sit ahead of any close this system has pulled; CL-0015 is the test built to answer that tomorrow and over the days that follow.

Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 3 of ~10

A rate spike isn't a risk spike until credit says so — using OAS as the tie-breaker between a “clean” Fed move and something breaking

Today's dashboard has two things happening at once that are easy to blur together: Treasury yields just posted this system's largest statistical flag ever (the 3-month bill), and stocks are still near all-time highs. A newer reader's instinct is to treat "yields spiking" as inherently scary. It isn't, on its own — and credit spreads are how you tell the difference. When yields rise because the Fed is expected to raise rates on a strong economy (today's case: the CPI print that triggered this move was hot because growth and prices are running warm, not because something is breaking), credit spreads — the extra yield lenders demand for default and liquidity risk — usually stay calm, because a strong economy is good for a borrower's ability to repay. That's exactly what's showing up right now: both high-yield and investment-grade OAS are unflagged, several standard deviations from any warning level. Contrast that with a rate spike that happens because markets are pricing recession or default risk (a "something's breaking" move): there, credit spreads widen right alongside yields, often faster. The practical rule: don't read a Treasury-yield move as a risk signal by itself. Check whether credit is confirming it. Today, it isn't — which is the specific, falsifiable reason this system's regime tag stays "tightening" and not "stress."

Client translation: "Rates jumping because the Fed might raise them isn't the same kind of scary as rates jumping because something's breaking — the tell is whether corporate borrowing costs are moving too, and right now they're not."

The reassuring half of today's data: corporate borrowing costs (credit spreads) haven't moved at all, meaning the market isn't pricing this as a threat to the broader economy or to corporate America's ability to pay its debts — see today's concept, above.
