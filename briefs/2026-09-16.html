Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 5 of ~10

Why flat credit spreads heading into a known event are informative, not just quiet

Every earlier segment in this unit used OAS (the extra yield lenders demand over Treasuries for default and liquidity risk) to tell a real risk event apart from a false positive — a rate spike, a violent single-stock swing. Today hands a different, related case: a known, scheduled event (a Fed decision, with a probability the market has been pricing for weeks) sits a few hours away, and credit spreads are essentially flat. It would be easy to read that as "nothing to see" — but flat spreads heading into a scheduled catalyst are themselves a data point, not an absence of one. Corporate bond investors, like equity investors, can price a known event in advance; if today's decision were likely to surprise the market in a way that raised real default or liquidity risk — an unexpectedly large hike, a shock to the dot plot — credit would typically start drifting wider in the days before it, the way it sometimes does ahead of a contested earnings report or a close election. It hasn't: HY OAS ticked up 6bp but stays two-thirds of a standard deviation below its own trailing average, and IG OAS is flat. That is consistent with a decision the market genuinely believes is close to fully priced, which is a different (and generally lower-risk) situation than a coin-flip event credit hasn't yet reacted to. The distinction matters for tomorrow morning specifically: if the Fed delivers something close to what's priced, credit staying calm today is exactly what you'd expect in hindsight; if the Fed surprises hard in either direction, watch whether credit reacts alongside rates — that reaction, not the rate move alone, is what would tell you whether markets are pricing new risk or just repricing the path.

Client translation: "Corporate bond markets aren't nervous heading into today's Fed decision — and that's useful information, because it means professional lenders think this is priced in and won't cause real financial stress, which is different from an unexpected shock."
