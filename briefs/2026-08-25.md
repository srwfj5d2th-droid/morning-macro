## The story

This morning's dominant story is one sector, not the whole tape: memory and storage chipmakers are getting hit again. Micron (−5.8%), SanDisk (−6.5%) and Seagate (−6.5%) are all down together, and reporting points to one shared cause — word that the administration may let Apple buy DRAM from China's CXMT and NAND flash from China's YMTC, two state-backed Chinese chipmakers largely locked out of top-tier U.S. supply chains until now (Yahoo Finance, TipRanks). That's a competitive threat, not a demand signal — a question of who gets paid for next year's memory, not whether enough memory gets bought. Piling on: Samsung fell ~9% after its own shareholder-return plan disappointed investors, weighing on sentiment across the global memory complex (TipRanks). All three capped names share this one story — informative in itself: a sector-wide repricing of one new risk, not three separate company problems.

The pivot: does the rest of the data confirm this is bigger than one sector's bad morning? Not yet. Long rates — this month's main driver — actually eased today (10Y −4bp to 4.70%, 30Y −4bp to 5.23%), so today's chip weakness isn't a rates story. Junk-bond spreads, the fastest read on credit stress anywhere including AI-adjacent borrowers, are unchanged and calm. The broader semiconductor index (SMH) fell 2.4% but that single day isn't even statistically unusual against its own history (z +0.23). And the equal-weight S&P 500 is once again flagged as unusually strong versus its own history — most of the market outside the chip complex is fine. So far: a real, attributable competitive-risk headline for U.S. memory, contained to that sector — not yet a broader AI-capex or credit event.

## Concept of the day — Unit 1 · segment 5

**Duration — why the far end of the curve swings harder for the same move.** Today the 10Y and 30Y both eased by the same 4bp — but that identical yield move doesn't produce an identical price move, and the reason is duration: how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in yield. Duration roughly tracks how long you wait, on average, to get your money back through interest payments; the longer the wait, the more the price swings for a given yield change, because more of the bond's value sits far off in a future that just got repriced. A 2-year note's duration runs near 2; a 30-year bond's can run past 15. That's why the 30Y carries a bigger flag today (+1.72) than the 10Y (+1.52) even though both are simply "elevated versus their own six-month history" — the far end has more duration, so the same story shows up louder there. It's also why a bond fund reacts so much more than a money-market fund to the same Fed-driven move: duration is the dial that sets how loud the news gets played.

**Client translation:** "Two bonds can react to the exact same interest-rate news by very different amounts — the longer the bond, the bigger the price swing. That's why your long-term bond fund moves so much more than your money-market fund on days like today, even though the same rate story drives both."
