The story

This morning's press narrative runs on two tracks that turn out to be the same story told twice. First, rates: CNBC and TechTimes both frame Tuesday as the day fed-funds-futures odds of a quarter-point September hike jumped to roughly 68%, up from about 40% a week ago, as the market keeps re-pricing Fed Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole tone from Friday. Second, and downstream of the first: Benzinga's markets desk describes Tuesday's slide in high-multiple software names — CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Datadog among them — as "rising Treasury yields... crushed high-multiple software stocks, not any fundamental weakness," with the 10-year's push toward 4.8% cited directly as the mechanism. Layered on top, Bloomberg reports fresh U.S. strikes on Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz overnight, with crossings through the strait reportedly down sharply from a week ago — the reason oil is up again this morning.

Does the state file confirm the rates story? Yes, more clearly than at any point this cycle. All four Treasury tenors this system tracks are flagged together for a second straight session, and the 10-year is today's largest single flag (z +2.06 — higher than all but a handful of days in the past six months) with the 2-year, 30-year, and 3-month right behind it (z +1.91, +1.93, +1.66). SOFR, the overnight funding rate that anchors floating-rate loans, is flagged too (z +1.51), and the 3-month/10-year spread — a classic recession-risk gauge — widened to a level this system also flags (z +1.56), which on its own would normally read as growth optimism re-pricing rather than stress; paired with everything else here it reads as "the whole curve moved up together," not a shape change. Does it confirm the software story? The correlation, at least: Tuesday's software decline (see Movers, below) landed on the same day the 10-year pushed to its highest level in this system's history, and Benzinga's reporting attributes the move directly to that mechanism. This brief treats "rates are driving the software selloff" as a hypothesis rather than a settled fact — it's logged as a new claim (CL-0009, below) rather than adopted into this brief's own voice. Credit, meanwhile, stayed calm — high-yield spreads actually tightened a touch (2.63%, z −1.10, near a six-month low) and investment-grade spreads barely budged (0.80%, z +0.20) — which is the detail that keeps today's tag at "tightening" rather than "stress": rates are repricing hard, but nothing in the credit market says anyone doubts anyone's ability to pay.

Concept of the day · Unit 2 · Real vs. nominal decomposition · segment 3 of roughly 6

The real yield has two pieces too: expected path and real term premium

The last two segments split the nominal 10-year yield into a real yield (TIPS) and a breakeven (implied inflation). Today, go one layer deeper: the real yield itself splits into two pieces — the expected path of real short-term rates (roughly, how tight the Fed will keep policy after backing out inflation) and the real term premium (the extra compensation investors demand for the risk and uncertainty of holding a 10-year bond instead of rolling over short-term ones). This matters for reading today's move specifically: the real yield has now risen for two straight sessions (2.42% Friday → 2.44% Monday, the most recent published pair) alongside rising fed-hike odds reported this morning (roughly 68%, up from 40% a week ago per CNBC/TechTimes) — that combination points toward the expected-path piece doing the work, not the term-premium piece, because a genuine path repricing is exactly what should happen when the market moves the odds of an actual Fed decision. This system can't yet decompose real yields into path versus premium numerically (that requires a term-structure model beyond what this system computes), so today's read is qualitative, not a script-verified split like the real/breakeven decomposition — flagged here as a limit of what this brief can currently prove, not a claim it's making with confidence.

Client translation: "Even the 'real' part of a bond yield has two pieces — how tight the Fed will actually be, and how much extra investors want for the uncertainty of a ten-year commitment — and right now the moves line up with the market believing the Fed, not just getting nervous about holding long bonds."
