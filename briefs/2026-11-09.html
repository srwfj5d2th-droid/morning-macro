The story

Friday's own coverage (The Washington Post, Fox Business, TheStreet) was straightforward: the Nasdaq rose 1.09% to 24,579.42 and the S&P 500 rose 0.31% to 7,376.86, a partial bounce from Thursday's rout, led by chip and AI-hardware names — Nvidia +2.8%, AMD +11%, SanDisk +nearly 4%. The Dow lagged, down about 0.46%. For the week, every major index still finished lower (Nasdaq −3.4%, S&P −2.7%, Dow −0.9%). The same morning brought the October jobs report: nonfarm payrolls +165K against a roughly 63K consensus, unemployment down to 4.1%, wages up 0.4% — a clean beat covered by Reuters, CNBC, and the BLS release itself. Multiple named outlets (Kitco, Morningstar, the South China Morning Post) tie that beat directly to a jump in December Fed rate-hike odds, to roughly 78–80% on CME FedWatch and prediction markets, up from about 67% before the print — the opposite of what a hot labor report would mean for cut odds, and consistent with the Oct. 27–28 FOMC's own 10–2 hold, where two dissents favored a hike outright.

This system's own pull backs those numbers and adds the piece the headlines treat as separate stories. The jobs beat and the rate-complex reaction aren't two stories; they're one. The 2-year yield rose 5bp to 4.51% Friday — more than the 10-year's 2bp (to 4.86%) or the 30-year's 1bp (to 5.26%) — a front-end-led move that flattened 2s10s by 3bp to 35bp, exactly the pattern Unit 1 (taught in August) says to expect when hot data represses the Fed's expected path rather than just nudging the term premium: the front end, not the long end, does the repricing. The dollar firmed 0.74% to 102.30 (newly flagged, z +1.64) in the same hawkish-repricing direction. But credit didn't confirm the equity bounce. HY OAS and IG OAS — both already flagged Thursday — widened again on Thursday's own print (dated with the usual one-day lag): HY OAS z +2.83 (was +2.18), IG OAS z +2.78 (was +2.44). Per this brief's own epistemics rule (§4C) and Unit 8's hierarchy (taught this week): when equities and credit disagree, credit gets the benefit of the doubt. Friday's bounce is real; it just isn't yet confirmed.

Two of the day's three qualifying movers were earnings- or analyst-driven, not drift: Axon fell 18.18% on an outright Q3 miss and cut guidance, while Coherent rallied 13.58% on a same-day Citi price-target raise following Thursday's post-earnings drop — a reminder that Thursday's “beat can still be a sell” lesson (Unit 6) can also run in reverse two sessions later once analysts re-price the same numbers (Movers, below).

The front end of the curve did almost all of Friday's work, and it moved in the direction a hot jobs report points toward. The 2-year yield — the part of the curve that tracks Fed expectations most closely — rose 5bp to 4.51% (z +0.52; 49th percentile since 1977 but the 77th of the last 30 years, † — cite both). The 3-month bill rose 2bp to 4.19% (z +1.26, not flagged; 52nd/70th percentile, no real gap). The 10-year rose 2bp to 4.86% (z +0.39; 45th/81st, †) and the 30-year rose just 1bp to 5.26% (z +0.25; 46th/80th, † — today's lead History-check fact, below). Why this matters: when short rates move more than long ones on a single data point, the bond market is repricing how many more times the Fed moves, not how much growth or inflation risk gets priced in for the next decade — the kind of move that can nudge a new CD or a short-term savings rate before it touches a 30-year mortgage quote.

The curve itself flattened a touch on that front-end move: 2s10s narrowed 3bp to 35bp, 3m10y held flat at 67bp — both still comfortably positive, far from the inversion that shows up on this morning's longer-run watch-list (History, below).

Credit, by contrast, widened again — the day's real divergence. HY OAS (the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated companies, dated Thursday 11-05, the usual one-day lag) rose 16bp to 3.48% (z +2.83, flagged, now this run's largest Tier 1 flag; 87th percentile of its own ~3-year record, §4F, marked *). IG OAS, the investment-grade equivalent (also dated 11-05), rose 2bp to 0.93% (z +2.78, flagged; 74th percentile of its own ~3-year record, also *). SOFR, the overnight bank-to-bank borrowing rate (also dated 11-05), eased 2bp to 3.92% (z +1.71, still flagged; 45th percentile since 1954 but the 67th of the last 30 years, †). Why this matters: a credit spread that widens on a day stocks rise is credit pricing in more default/liquidity risk even as equity sentiment improves — lenders aren't buying the relief rally yet, and historically that disagreement is worth more weight than the equity move alone.

The dollar (DXY) joined the hawkish-repricing side of the ledger: it rose 0.74% to 102.30 (newly flagged, z +1.64; 72nd/81st percentile, no real gap — cite either). Fed balance-sheet and TGA readings are still last Thursday's H.4.1/DTS release, carried forward unchanged — thin history, no pre-2008 comparison exists for either series (§4F). ON RRP, the nearly drained overnight-cash shock absorber, ticked up to $0.18B (z −0.53, not flagged). Why this matters: a strengthening dollar on a hot jobs print is the same signal as the front-end yield rise — markets pricing a Fed that holds higher for longer, which tightens financial conditions globally even without the Fed lifting a finger today.

Concept of the day · Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 3

When agreement doesn't last — reapplying the hierarchy the very next session

Segment 2 showed the clean case: Thursday, stocks, credit, and bonds all moved together, and agreement across markets that don't have to agree was itself the strongest signal available. Friday hands the unit the next lesson, and it's an uncomfortable one: that agreement didn't survive one session. Stocks bounced; credit widened again; the dollar and the front end of the curve moved in the hawkish direction on a jobs report that argued for a less generous Fed, not a more generous one. Segment 1's hierarchy — when markets disagree, weight credit over equity sentiment — isn't a one-time tool you use once and retire. It has to be reapplied every single morning, because yesterday's agreement is not a standing fact about today's market; it's a reading that can and does expire. The discipline this teaches isn't “credit is always right” — it's that confirmation has to be checked fresh each day, the same way this brief reconciles its claims ledger fresh each morning rather than assuming yesterday's conclusion still holds.

Client translation: Just because the market agreed with itself yesterday doesn't mean it still does today -- check the signal fresh every morning instead of assuming it's still true.

Client lens

“The market bounced back Friday, so are we past the worst of it?” Stocks did bounce, but the part of the market that tends to be the more reliable warning sign — credit spreads — got wider, not narrower, the same day. That disagreement, not the headline index number, is the thing worth watching into this week.

“I heard the jobs report was good — isn't that bullish?” A strong jobs report is good news about the economy, but it's being read by markets as raising the odds the Fed holds rates higher for longer (or even hikes in December), which is why short-term bond yields and the dollar rose Friday even as stocks rallied. Good economic news and a friendlier Fed don't always arrive together.

“What happened with Axon and Coherent?” Two unrelated, company-specific stories: Axon missed earnings and cut guidance (down 18%); Coherent, which had actually beaten its own earnings guidance Wednesday night but sold off anyway, got an analyst price-target upgrade Friday and partly recovered (up 14%). Neither is a read on the broader market — both are useful reminders that a single day's stock move can be about one company's numbers, not the macro picture.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
