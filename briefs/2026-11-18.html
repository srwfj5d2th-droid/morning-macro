The story

Tuesday's dominant financial-press narrative was a renewed slide in software stocks, not chips. Salesforce fell about 9% to $194.52, its worst day on this screen, with ServiceNow (−6.5% to $92.90), Workday, Datadog, and Oracle all down alongside it. GuruFocus and Pomegra tied the move to the same AI-disruption-and-monetization doubt that hit chip stocks earlier this month; Yahoo Finance quoted an RBC Capital Markets analyst warning that the AI companies are going to try to take Salesforce's business away entirely, and Pomegra linked the selling to Anthropic's roughly $64 billion IPO, pricing Thursday, pulling capital out of application-software names ahead of it. One outlet, Invezz, separately tied Salesforce's drop to a string of recent technology outages raising software-reliability worries — a claim this morning's search could not corroborate elsewhere, so it is reported single-sourced, not as settled fact. Intuit (−7.2%) and ServiceNow fell with no news of their own — Intuit's own earnings aren't due until Thursday after the close — a pattern of sympathy selling into an unreported earnings date (Unit 6's September lesson, applied live). Separately, after Tuesday's close, Nvidia topped Q3 estimates, announced a $150 billion buyback, and gave a Q4 outlook ahead of consensus — its first report since Jensen Huang's Nov. 11 resignation — and was trading up nearly 4% premarket Wednesday, per CNBC.

This system's own pull backs the press narrative more than it has in weeks. Unlike Monday, when credit stayed flat while equities and chips sold off, both high-yield and investment-grade spreads widened today and are back above their own flag lines (Dashboard, below) — the first time in over a week that credit has moved the same direction as a stock selloff rather than sitting it out. Breadth confirms too: the equal-weight S&P (RSP) fell 1.45%, more than the S&P 500's own 1.03% — a reversal from the narrow, mega-cap-driven selling of the past two weeks (Unit 6's breadth tool, reactivated). Today's decliners ran wide: software (Salesforce, Intuit, ServiceNow), healthcare (UnitedHealth, −5.4%), cell-tower REITs (SBAC and CCI, both off more than 5.8% as real yields climbed — Dashboard, below), and consumer names (Nike, Best Buy). Rates, the dollar, credit, and now breadth are, for the first time in over a week, all leaning the same tightening direction at once — the live case for Unit 8's capstone, below. The S&P 500's 1.03% drop is itself an ordinary-sized move (one this big happens about 33 times a year over the last 30 years, and it's the 24th such day this year; Monday's own 1.9% drop was bigger). It's the number of things agreeing today, not the size of any one of them, that makes this morning worth a second look.

Every Treasury tenor eased a touch today, but the front end stayed the dashboard's most flagged corner. The 3-month bill eased 1bp to 4.33% but is still up 9bp over five sessions (z +1.83, flagged; 54th percentile since the early 1980s but the 72nd of the last 30 years). The 2-year eased 3bp to 4.80% but is up 24bp over five sessions (z +1.42, just below the flag line; 53rd percentile over the full 50-year record but the 83rd of the last 30 years, † a 30-point gap). The 10-year eased 2bp to 5.10% (z +1.21, below the flag line; 47th/85th %ile, † today's lead History-check fact, below) and the 30-year held flat at 5.46% (z +1.17; 48th/84th %ile, †). The curve itself barely moved (2s10s +1bp to 30bp; 3m10y −1bp to 77bp, both comfortably un-inverted, neither flagged). Why this matters: every tenor easing slightly on a day stocks fell hard is itself informative — a genuine flight-to-Treasuries bid would have pushed yields down harder than a couple of basis points. This reads as a small pause inside a longer climb, not a reversal: closer to a CD rate holding steady than a mortgage rate actually easing.

Credit, by contrast, didn't just hold — it moved, and it moved with equities. HY OAS (dated Monday 11-16, the usual one-session lag) widened 11bp to 334bp (z +1.85, flagged; 81st percentile of its own ~3-year record, marked *, since this live series has no multi-decade history — see §4F). IG OAS (also dated 11-16) ticked up 1bp to 90bp (z +1.79, flagged; 63rd percentile of its own ~3-year record, marked *). Both tiers of credit easing together was last week's story; both widening together is today's — the first time since late October that credit has leaned the same direction as a stock selloff instead of sitting it out. SOFR (also dated 11-16) rose 3bp to 3.93% (z +1.64, flagged; 45th percentile over the full 72-year record but the 68th of the last 30 years, †). The dollar eased slightly on the day (DXY −0.06 to 103.49) but is still up 1.26 points over five sessions and carries this run's single largest Tier 1 flag (z +2.36; 74th percentile over the full 55-year record but the 84th of the last 30 years, no real gap). The 10-year TIPS real yield (dated Monday) rose 1bp to 2.92%, up 29bp over five sessions (z +1.83, flagged; 99.7th percentile of its own 24-year TIPS record — but the Cleveland Fed's longer model, back to 1982, puts real rates at just the 80th percentile of the last 30 years and the 54th since 1982, a materially milder read using the same concept). The 10-year breakeven eased 4bp to 2.20% (z −1.81, flagged; 48th percentile of its own 24-year record, about the middle historically even as it's flagged lately). Why this matters: when credit, the market's own read on default risk, starts moving in the same direction as a stock selloff instead of shrugging it off, that's the signal worth taking most seriously on this page — not because any single number here is extreme, but because the whole hierarchy just started pointing the same way at once.

Concept of the day · Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 8 (capstone) — curriculum complete

When all four votes finally agree — the hierarchy-of-evidence habit, applied end to end, closes the eight-unit curriculum

Segments 1 through 7 built the hierarchy one piece at a time: rates, credit, liquidity, the dollar, breadth, financing structure, and then cross-asset confirmation itself — rates, credit, the dollar, and equities, with commodities folded in as a mostly-dollar echo rather than a fifth independent vote (segment 7). Most days, these votes split: credit shrugs while equities sell off, or the dollar and gold both fall together for the same dollar-pricing reason rather than two separate opinions. Today is the exception that makes the habit useful. Both HY and IG OAS widened alongside the stock selloff for the first time in over a week; breadth broke the same direction too, with the equal-weight S&P falling more than the cap-weighted index for the first time in two weeks (Story, above); the dollar and rates were already leaning tight. Four votes, one direction, on a day whose single biggest number — the S&P's 1.03% drop — is itself unremarkable (a move that size happens about 33 times a year).

That's the whole point of building the habit over eight units: the size of any one number matters less than whether the others agree with it, and today several of them finally did. This closes the curriculum's eight-unit sequence — the dual endpoint from §7 is now in place: reading the data like an analyst (the hierarchy itself) and explaining it like an advisor (the client-translation line below). From here, the brief reinforces these eight tools against live data rather than introducing new ones.

Client translation: When your statement looks rough and the news, the bond market, and credit spreads are all telling the same story at once, that's worth paying more attention to than any one of them alone — even on a day when no single number looks historically dramatic.

Client lens

“Why did Salesforce fall 9% with no earnings?” It wasn't company news — it's part of a broader worry that AI tools could eventually take business away from software companies like Salesforce, not a one-day story. Intuit and ServiceNow fell the same day on the same theme, with no news of their own.

“Is this the same AI selloff as the chip stocks a few weeks ago?” Related, but not identical. The chip selloff was about whether companies keep spending on AI data centers and chips. This week's move is about whether AI tools disrupt the software companies themselves — a different worry, inside the same broad AI-capex debate.

“Should I be more worried this time?” This is the first day in over a week where rates, credit spreads, the dollar, and the stock market have all leaned the same direction together — worth watching, though no individual number today is historically extreme. We'll keep checking whether it holds tomorrow, and into this afternoon's Fed minutes.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
