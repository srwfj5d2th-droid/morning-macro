**Reading a real raise through the Minsky lens — hedge, speculative, or Ponzi financing.** Segment 1 introduced Hyman Minsky's three kinds of financing: hedge (a company's own cash flow comfortably covers what it owes), speculative (cash flow covers the interest, but the principal has to be refinanced when it's due), and Ponzi (cash flow doesn't even cover the interest, so new financing or rising asset values have to keep arriving just to service the old financing). Oracle's $20 billion raise (Movers, above) is a clean, live chance to practice sorting a real filing into one of those three buckets — except this system's own data pull doesn't include Oracle's operating cash flow or interest-coverage detail today, so it can't actually answer the question with confidence, and won't pretend to. What it can do is name the exact question that answer would settle: does Oracle's existing business already generate enough cash to cover the new $10 billion note's interest payments on its own (hedge-like), or does the AI buildout itself have to start paying off before the debt comes due (speculative)? The equity half of the raise sidesteps the question for that $10 billion specifically — stock never has to be repaid — but it dilutes existing shareholders either way, which is its own kind of cost. Knowing which question to ask, and knowing this system doesn't yet have the data to answer it, is the actual skill Unit 7 is building: a financing-cycle read is a diagnostic, not a verdict rendered from a headline.

**Client translation:** "When a company borrows and sells new stock to pay for a big expansion, the question that matters is whether its regular business already earns enough to cover the new debt payments on its own -- that's what separates ordinary growth financing from a bet that needs everything to keep working."
