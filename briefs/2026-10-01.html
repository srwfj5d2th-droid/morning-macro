Concept of the day · Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, segment 5

The quarter-end turn — why ON RRP usage jumps even in a “drained” facility, once a quarter

Segment 4 called the ON RRP facility a nearly-drained shock absorber — usage had fallen to a few hundred million dollars for weeks. Today's own number (Dashboard, above) jumped to $11.54 billion in two sessions. That is not new cash flooding into the system; it is the quarter-end turn, a well-documented, recurring event that happens every March, June, September, and December 31st (or the last business day before it). Banks report their balance sheets to regulators at quarter-end, and one of the ratios they're graded on (the supplementary leverage ratio) counts total assets, repo loans included, regardless of how safe those loans are. So for a few days around quarter-end, banks and bond dealers temporarily shrink their repo-lending books to look smaller and better-capitalized on that one snapshot date — even though nothing about their actual risk changed. Money-market funds that would normally lend that cash to dealers overnight suddenly have fewer willing borrowers, so they default to lending it to the one counterparty that always takes it: the Fed's own ON RRP facility. It is a balance-sheet costume change, not a liquidity event, and it reliably unwinds within a day or two once the new quarter's reporting date has passed — worth knowing so a future spike doesn't get mistaken for a repo-market problem.

Client translation: The giant one-day jump in cash parked at the Fed isn't new money sitting on the sidelines — it's banks temporarily tidying up their books for a quarter-end report card, and it normally unwinds within a few days.

Client lens

“Jabil beat earnings and raised guidance — why did the stock drop 10%?” Likely to come up given the size of the move (Movers, above). Good numbers aren't the same as good-enough numbers: the stock was already priced for a strong quarter, so beating by a little wasn't enough to keep it from falling — a reminder that "how much was already expected" matters as much as the headline result.

“Inflation came in cooler than expected yesterday — so why are mortgage rates and bond yields still so high?” Timely given yesterday's Core PCE miss (Story, above) and the still-elevated dashboard (above). Because this month's move was never mainly about inflation fears to begin with — this system's own data has shown for weeks that real rates (the part of a bond yield left over after inflation) have done almost all of the work, not inflation expectations. A cooler inflation print doesn't undo a real-rate move; it just confirms that particular worry wasn't the one driving yields up.

“Is this a credit crunch starting, like 2008?” A natural question given today's credit-broadening confirmation (Claims, Dashboard, above) and the press's own 2007/2002 yield comparisons (Story, above). Not based on what this system can see: both high-yield and investment-grade spreads are still well below where they sat even earlier this year, equities and short-term funding markets are calm, and there's no credit event behind the move — just rates finally showing up in how lenders price risk. Worth watching, not worth alarm; this system will keep grading it claim by claim rather than calling it from a headline.
