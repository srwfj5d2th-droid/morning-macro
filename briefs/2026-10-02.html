Does this system's own data confirm that "stocks found their footing" framing, or complicate it? It complicates it. Yes, the whole Treasury curve eased for a second session (Dashboard, below) — that part of the press framing checks out, and CL-0026 above confirms it was the cooler PCE print doing the easing. But credit didn't ease with it. HY OAS (the extra yield lenders demand for junk-rated debt) widened again to 3.12%, its widest reading in this system's entire history (z +4.88 — more extreme than all but a handful of days in six months of data, Dashboard below), and IG OAS (the same measure for highly-rated debt) stayed flagged too. That's the real tension this morning: rates eased like something is cooling, while credit widened like something is still tightening. This system is keeping the regime tag at "tightening," not moving it to "stress," because the things a genuine stress call would need aren't here — equities barely moved (S&P 500 +0.2%, Recap above), funding markets are calm (the ON RRP spike that worried Wednesday's dashboard fully unwound overnight, Concept below, exactly the quarter-end costume change it was explained as), and there's no forced-selling signature anywhere in the data. But a rates-easing, credit-widening split is unusual enough that it's worth saying plainly rather than waving away, and this morning's jobs report (Claims, above) is a live test of which side is reading the economy correctly.

Concept of the day · Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, segment 6

The quarter-end turn, verified — how fast window dressing actually unwinds

Yesterday's segment called the ON RRP facility's $11.54 billion two-day spike a balance-sheet costume change, not a liquidity event — banks temporarily shrinking their repo-lending books to look smaller on a regulatory snapshot date, with the prediction that it would unwind within days once the new quarter's reporting date had passed. Today's own number (Dashboard, above) didn't wait days: ON RRP fell from $11.54 billion back to $0.35 billion in a single session, essentially all the way back to where it sat before the spike. That speed is itself the useful information. A real liquidity problem — genuinely scarce bank reserves, or dealers unable to find cash — doesn't resolve in one day just because the calendar flipped; it takes real money moving, which takes time. A regulatory snapshot effect, by contrast, is mechanically tied to one date on the calendar, so once that date passes, the behavior that caused it has no reason to continue, and it unwinds immediately. The speed of an unwind is a diagnostic in its own right: slow-motion reversals look like real stress working itself out, and one-day round-trips look like exactly what this one was.

Client translation: That overnight-cash spike at the Fed wasn't new money piling in on the sidelines — it was banks tidying their books for one regulatory snapshot date, and the fact that it reversed in a single day is itself the proof.
