The story

The press is leading with the long bond. TheStreet's Tuesday wrap ran under "Nasdaq, S&P 500 slip as 30-year Treasury yield hits highest point in nearly two decades," and this morning's coverage (Yahoo Finance) has futures stalling after the White House paused tariffs on Canadian goods, with Asia sharply lower — Korea's KOSPI down more than 5%, led by the memory complex. The circulating narrative: yields are spiking and the AI trade is cracking.

The state file mostly confirms the rates half — with one correction. The 30Y at 5.28% carries a z-score of +2.2 against its own trailing 120 days, +44bp YTD; the 10Y at 4.71% is +1.6σ; and the 10Y real yield (2.44%, Monday's print) is +1.6σ while the 10Y breakeven sits at a sleepy 2.28% (z −0.6). That decomposition matters: this is a real-rate repricing, not an inflation scare (logged as CL-0003). The correction: on a closing basis the long end actually eased Tuesday — 30Y −3bp from Monday's 5.31% peak, 10Y −1bp — so "yields spiked Tuesday" describes the intraday tape, not the close. The narrative is a week old; Tuesday was the equity market catching up to it.

And the equity damage was surgical, not systemic. The S&P fell 0.69% but the equal-weight index fell only 0.45% — the average stock outperformed the cap-weighted index on a down day — while SMH dropped 4.09% and Energy, Health Care, and Staples all finished green. HY OAS sat at 2.70% (z −0.8, 11bp tighter YTD) and IG at 0.81%: credit, the asset class that panics first when something is actually breaking, did nothing. The data reads as a crowded-trade unwind priced off higher discount rates — hypothesis CL-0002, graded within the week — not broad risk-off. The minutes at 2pm are the next test (CL-0001).

Concept of the day · Unit 1 · Yield curve mechanics · segment 1 of ~10

The curve is a forecast, not a price list

Start with what a Treasury yield actually is: the market-clearing price of time. This morning the overnight rate (SOFR) is 3.65% — that one the Fed effectively administers. But the 2Y at 4.19%, the 10Y at 4.71%, the 30Y at 5.28% — nobody sets those. Each is an auction-priced forecast: the average overnight rate the market expects over that horizon, plus a premium for bearing the uncertainty of holding paper that long. So when this week's headlines say "rates hit a two-decade high," read it precisely: the Fed has done nothing; the market has raised the price of long-dated time. The gap between the 2Y and 10Y — 2s10s, +52bp this morning — is the curve's slope, and its direction is a sentence: a steepening curve with the front end pinned (exactly this week's shape) says "policy is where we thought, but holding long bonds now requires more compensation." Every curve move decomposes into those two parts — expected path, and premium — and telling them apart is the next segment's job.

Client translation: "When you hear 'rates hit a 20-year high,' notice the Fed hasn't raised anything — the market repriced long-term borrowing. That's why your money-market yield didn't move this week, but long bond funds did."
