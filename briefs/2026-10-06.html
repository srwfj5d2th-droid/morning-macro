Concept of the day · Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, capstone

The liquidity-plumbing checklist — reserves, TGA, RRP, and SOFR as one system, not four separate numbers

Eight segments in, the pieces finally combine into one mental model. Bank reserves — the cash banks keep at the Fed, the actual fuel for day-to-day lending — equal the Fed's balance sheet minus the Treasury's checking account (the TGA) minus money funds' overnight parking spot (ON RRP). Four different things can move that number, for four different reasons: the Fed buying or letting bonds roll off (QT — slow and deliberate); the Treasury building cash ahead of a refunding (fast, then reverses once spent); a quarter-end regulatory snapshot (one day, mechanical, reverses immediately); or a Treasury auction settling (a specific date, a session or two after the auction itself — not the auction day). SOFR — the overnight rate banks actually charge each other — is the gauge that tells you reserves got scarce; it's flagged again today (z +2.88), but this system's own tracking says that's still the aging post-hike repricing from September, not a fresh squeeze. The checklist for any future SOFR or reserves move: which of the four causes is it, is it a one-day mechanical effect or a multi-day real one, and does the direction match what the calendar (Treasury auctions, Fed meetings, quarter-ends) would predict? That's the whole unit compressed into one habit.

Client translation: When the news says the Fed's balance sheet shrank or the Treasury's cash pile grew, the right question isn't "is that bad" — it's "which of four known plumbing events caused it, and does it reverse on its own."
