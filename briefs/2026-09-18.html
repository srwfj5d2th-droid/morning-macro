The story

Reporting this morning centers on the rebound. CNBC and TheStreet report U.S. equities climbed Thursday as Treasury yields and oil retreated, clawing back Wednesday's post-Fed selloff — the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite both closed higher, led by "Magnificent Seven" names Nvidia and Amazon (both up over 2%) and Microsoft (+1.5%). The sharpest move was in chips: 24/7 Wall St. and TradingKey report Intel jumped roughly 8%, AMD 7%, and Micron over 5%, in what both outlets explicitly frame as a technical, positioning-driven squeeze — "explosive call [options] activity" in Intel and dip-buying in Micron and AMD forcing short sellers to cover, a third straight session of the rebound — rather than a fresh fundamental reassessment of the AI trade. Separately, Bloomberg, Benzinga, and The Daily Upside report Amazon signed a seven-year, up-to-$8 billion supply agreement for Generac backup power generators for its AI data centers, with the first $2.4 billion of deliveries set for 2027-2028 and Amazon receiving warrants on roughly 1.7 million Generac shares as part of the structure — sending Generac to its best single-day gain on record (up as much as 27% intraday per Bloomberg). And the Washington Times reports weekly jobless claims fell to 196,000, the fewest since mid-July, a sign of continued labor-market resilience even after Wednesday's hike.

Does the state file confirm the press's framing? On the rebound itself, cleanly: the S&P 500 rose 1.14% and the Nasdaq-100 1.69%, both real, broad moves. But the state file adds something the "market recovers" framing alone doesn't: credit's first same-day reaction to Wednesday's actual decision. High-yield and investment-grade spreads — both now dated Wednesday 09-16, the decision day itself — eased rather than widened (Dashboard, below), the opposite of what a "tightening financial conditions" reading of the hawkish dot plot would predict. That is a genuine complication of the loudest version of Wednesday's "hawkish surprise" story, not a confirmation of Thursday's "relief rally" framing either — credit was never rattled in the first place. On the chip rebound, the state file leans toward the technical read reporting gave it: SMH jumped 2.76%, narrowing CL-0015's SMH-vs-SPY gap to its tightest reading yet (-1.17pp, Claims above), but the front-end Treasury market told a matching story — the 2-year gave back 7bp of Wednesday's move, a partial fade of the very hawkish surprise chip traders were reportedly squeezing out of, not an independent AI-conviction signal. Both threads point the same direction: Thursday looks like digestion and positioning unwinding a sharp Wednesday move, not a new piece of information.

Credit's first same-day read on the decision itself: calmer, not more stressed. Concept of the day · Unit 3 · Credit spreads · segment 7 of ~10

A cushion, not just calm — credit tightening on a hawkish surprise is its own signal, distinct from merely staying flat

Segment 6 (yesterday) drew the line between credit staying flat ahead of a known event (informative on its own) and credit's actual reaction once the event lands (the real test). Today's fill answers that test, and answers it more sharply than a simple pass: on Wednesday, the day the Fed delivered a rate hike plus a hawkish dot plot that press coverage called the real surprise (Story, above), high-yield spreads didn't just hold steady — they eased 6bp, and investment-grade spreads eased 2bp. That distinction matters. Flat credit says lenders saw nothing new to reprice. Tightening credit says something stronger: that on a day risk should have looked marginally worse (a central bank signaling more restriction ahead, which should raise the odds that some weaker borrowers eventually struggle to refinance), the market that prices exactly that risk decided borrowers looked marginally safer instead. One honest limit, stated rather than glossed over: OAS is a single-day, aggregate number, and one day of tightening is a data point, not a trend — which is exactly what today's new claim (CL-0019) is built to test using the next fill. The lesson generalizes beyond this week: credit's direction on the day of a hawkish surprise, not just its level beforehand, is itself a piece of evidence.

Client translation: "The Fed just signaled it's not done raising rates, but the part of the market that actually lends money to companies got a little more comfortable, not less — that's a genuinely reassuring signal, though one day of it isn't proof the coast is clear."
