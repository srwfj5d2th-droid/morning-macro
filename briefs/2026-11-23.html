The story

Friday extended this month's AI-led unwind into a second, sharper leg. The S&P 500 fell 3.70% to 6,910.39, the Nasdaq-100 fell 5.28%, and the index closed 11.62% below its October 6 record — a confirmed correction. Coverage (amgreatness.com, “AI Sell-Off Drags Dow, S&P 500 Into Correction as Nasdaq's Gains for the Year Vanish”) framed the week as the S&P 500's worst percentage loss since April 2025, tying it to the same AI/chip-stock doubts that opened this selloff on Nov. 5. No weekend news of substance turned up in today's search — this Monday edition is built from Friday's own session plus today's calendar, nothing more invented to fill the gap.

This system's own pull confirms the broad framing but sharpens where the damage actually landed. Small caps (Russell 2000, −2.76%) and the equal-weight S&P (RSP, −1.98%, now itself flagged for the first time in weeks) both fell less than the cap-weighted indexes — real pain, but not as concentrated in a handful of giants as Friday's headline number suggests. Chips absorbed far more than their share: SMH fell 10.60%, and the Technology sector ETF (XLK, −7.27%) diverged from the S&P (−3.64%) by 3.63 percentage points, the day's only sector move of that size — every other sector ETF (staples, health care, energy, utilities, real estate, financials) actually outperformed the index (Movers, below). That argues for “a severe but concentrated tech/AI unwind” over “a market-wide panic,” even on a day this bad. Treasurys rallied across every tenor — the 10-year eased to 4.92%, the 30-year to 5.31%, a level that, before this run, was last touched in June 2007 (History, below) — and credit stayed wide (HY and IG OAS both still flagged). The dollar, by contrast, barely moved (DXY −0.09%): three of this system's four hierarchy votes leaned risk-off together, but the fourth just sat still, a reminder that Friday's agreement has to be read fresh rather than assumed to match Thursday's cleaner four-way case.

Why this matters: a bond is a loan, and its yield is what the borrower pays to borrow for that long — Treasury yields falling across every maturity on a day stocks fell hard is the clearest single piece of evidence that Friday was a genuine flight-to-safety move, not just money rotating out of one sector into another.

Credit stayed wide, the dollar barely moved, and gold and copper fell anyway — without the dollar doing the work this time. HY OAS — the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated companies over safe Treasurys — widened 5bp to 3.52% (dated Thursday 11-19, the usual one-session lag; z +2.37, flagged; 88th percentile of its own ~3-year record, marked * since this live series has no multi-decade history, §4F). IG OAS, the investment-grade equivalent, rose 1bp to 0.94% (also dated Thursday; z +2.40, flagged; 76th percentile of its own ~3-year record, marked *). The dollar (DXY) was essentially flat at 103.81 (−0.09%; z +2.41, this run's largest Tier 1 flag on a running basis; 75th percentile over the full ~55-year dollar record and the 84th of the last 30 years). Gold fell 4.62% to $3,515.40 (z −3.57, its third straight flagged session) and copper fell 2.63% (z −4.40) — both falling hard, but this time not because the dollar did it (Concept, below): with DXY barely moved, Friday's drop in two assets with very different reputations (one a traditional safe haven, one an industrial-growth bellwether) reads as a broader liquidation that didn't spare either, not a repeat of last week's dollar-driven mechanism. Why this matters: when the usual explanation for a move stops fitting the day's own numbers, that's worth noticing before repeating it to a client as if nothing changed.

Concept of the day · Unit 8 — Cross-asset confirmation, segment 7 reinforcement (R)

Same assets falling, different reason — gold and copper's drop wasn't the dollar's doing this time

Segment 7 taught that gold and copper are dollar-priced, so when both fall together it's often the dollar doing the work, not an independent vote on growth or fear — Thursday's brief used exactly that mechanism to explain gold's drop. Today looks the same on the surface: gold fell 4.62% and copper fell 2.63%, both flagged for a third straight session. But the mechanism isn't the same. The dollar barely moved Friday (DXY −0.09%, essentially flat) while Treasurys rallied and credit stayed wide — the textbook flight-to-safety signature. Gold, the asset that's supposed to benefit most from exactly that signature, fell instead.

The habit worth building: check the mechanism fresh each session rather than assuming yesterday's explanation still applies because the price action looks similar. With the dollar not doing the work Friday, the honest read is a broader liquidation that didn't spare gold or copper either — not a repeat of last week's dollar-driven story, even though the two charts look alike.

Client translation: Gold fell again Friday, but don't assume it's the same "strong dollar" story as before — the dollar barely moved that day, which means something broader was pressuring even the traditional safe havens, worth knowing before a client asks why gold isn't protecting them this week.

Client lens

“Did you see what happened Friday? Are we in a crash?” The S&P 500 is now in a confirmed correction (11.62% below its October record), but it's still up 0.95% for the year — worth saying plainly, since “correction” and “the year is a loss” aren't the same thing yet. We've got two live tests running (CL-0072, CL-0073; Claims/Flags, above) on whether this extends or echoes.

“Is this just a chip/AI problem, or is everything falling?” Both, in different degrees. Every major index fell Friday, but chips and the broader tech sector (SMH, XLK) fell far harder than the rest of the market, while sectors like staples, health care, utilities, and financials actually held up better than the index. It reads as a severe but concentrated unwind in AI-adjacent names, not an undifferentiated panic.

“Why did gold fall during such a scary week? Isn't that backwards?” Usually gold rises on days like Friday, and last week a strong dollar explained why it wasn't — but Friday the dollar barely moved, so that explanation doesn't hold this time (Concept, above). The honest answer is we don't have a clean mechanism for it yet; worth flagging that uncertainty rather than reaching for a tidy story.

Reading key
bp (basis point) = 1/100th of a percent — "+4bp" on a yield means it rose 0.04%.  z(120d) = how unusual today's level is versus the past ~6 months of trading days; 0 is typical, and anything beyond ±1.5 gets a ⚑ flag — far enough outside its recent normal range to deserve attention.  %ile all/30y = where today's level ranks against this series' real history — the all-time percentile, then the trailing-30-year percentile, since pooling 60+ years can hide a regime shift (a few extreme years can make "normal" look lower or higher than it's felt in the modern era) — 50 is the median either way; † flags when the two readings disagree by 20 points or more, which is itself the finding.  HY / IG OAS = the extra yield investors demand to lend to junk-rated (HY) or investment-grade (IG) companies instead of the government; widening = worry, calm = confidence — credit is the market's smoke detector.  2s10s / 3m10s = long-term Treasury yield minus short-term; the slope of the yield curve.  Regime tags: calm · risk-on/confirming · risk-on/diverging · tightening · stress.
