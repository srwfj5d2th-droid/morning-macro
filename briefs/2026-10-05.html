Credit spreads widened again for a third straight session — and stocks rallied right through it anyway.
risk-on/diverging

High-yield credit spreads — the extra interest junk-rated companies pay over the safer Treasury rate — widened again today. It's the widest level this system has measured in its own eight months of tracking, but that's a short yardstick, so here's a longer one: by the full historical record for this series (FRED, TradingEconomics), today's 3.24% is still below the 4.52% median spread since 1996, and far short of any real stress episode — the 2022 Fed-hiking cycle peaked near 6%, the 2015–16 energy bust hit roughly 8.7%, the 2020 COVID shock hit 10.9%, and the 2008 financial crisis peaked near 21.8%. This is a real, fast move worth watching, not a flashing red light yet. Meanwhile the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both rallied and the whole Treasury curve resumed climbing after two easier days — stocks and rates moving one way while credit moves the other is still why today reads “risk-on/diverging,” not another plain “tightening” day.

The story

Press framing this morning has one dominant thread. Bloomberg and CNBC report Wall Street heads into the week assessing whether Friday's rally — the Nasdaq's best session in weeks, per Bloomberg — can continue after a badly-missed September jobs report cut the odds of an October Fed rate hike from roughly 70% to the low-20s% (CME FedWatch data, widely reported). A second thread, smaller but live in this system's own numbers: Yahoo Finance, GuruFocus, and 24/7 Wall St report storage-drive makers Western Digital and Seagate each fell about 10% after rival Toshiba said it would double hard-disk production by fiscal 2027 — a direct challenge to the AI-data-center storage-scarcity trade that had carried both stocks up triple digits this year (Movers, below).

Does this system's own data confirm the "jobs miss clears the way for stocks" framing, or complicate it? It complicates it, and in a specific way worth sitting with. Equities did rally — the S&P 500 rose 0.73% and the Nasdaq 1.19% today, both real, measured moves (Dashboard, below). But the bond and credit markets did not confirm a simple "weak economy, lower rates, stocks love it" story. The whole Treasury curve actually resumed climbing after two easier sessions — every tenor this system tracks is flagged again — and high-yield credit spreads (the extra interest junk-rated companies pay to borrow) widened again, to the highest level this system has tracked in its own short history. Sized against the real historical record instead (Regime line, above), that's still a moderate level, not a stress one — but the direction and pace are real. A weak jobs report that doesn't bring yields down, on a day credit sends its loudest warning yet, while stocks rally anyway, is not "good news is good news" or "bad news is good news" — it's three signals pointing three different directions at once, and that disagreement is the actual story (new claim CL-0030, above).

One honest caveat on the jobs number itself: Friday's report is genuinely weak by any read — a 29,000 print against expectations near 84,000–98,000, with two months of downward revisions on top. The market's muted rate reaction (CL-0028, above) likely reflects that a December hike remains almost fully priced regardless — this was a timing shift, not a change in destination, which is a more measured outcome than the headline miss alone would suggest.

Concept of the day · Unit 4 — Liquidity plumbing, segment 7

Settlement day, not auction day — when a Treasury auction actually drains reserves

Segment 3 gave the reserves identity: bank reserves ≈ the Fed's balance sheet minus the Treasury's checking account (the TGA) minus the overnight-parking facility (ON RRP). An auction announcement doesn't move that identity by itself — what moves it is settlement, the day the Treasury actually collects cash from the winning bidders, which usually lands one or two business days after the auction itself, not on it. Until settlement, the TGA hasn't actually grown; the government has promised to receive money, but hasn't received it yet. This week gives a live test: Wednesday's 10-year and Thursday's 30-year auctions (Calendar, above) are each followed by a settlement date a session or two later — that's when to watch the TGA line in Friday's weekly H.4.1 release, not the auction days themselves. It's also the opposite case from segment 6's quarter-end spike: that was a one-day regulatory snapshot effect that reversed itself immediately once the date passed. A settlement-driven TGA build is the government actually holding more cash, pulled out of the banking system's reserves — it only reverses once the Treasury spends that cash back out, which takes real time, not a single session.

Client translation: When you hear the Treasury had a big bond auction this week, the thing to watch isn't auction day itself — it's a day or two later, once the government actually collects the cash, that you'd see any tightening ripple through day-to-day funding markets.
